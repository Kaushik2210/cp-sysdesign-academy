## Intuition

Some problems cannot be solved by walking left to right keeping a small state, because the best decision about the *middle* of a segment affects what is allowed on both sides. Take the question "in what order should I merge a row of piles to pay the least?" The first merge you do tells you almost nothing. The **last** merge is far more informative: it must join a left part and a right part, and those two parts were each built independently, in their own best way.

This is the shape of an **interval DP**: the state is a **segment** `[i, j]`, and the answer for a segment is assembled from answers for smaller segments inside it. Instead of asking "what do I do first?", ask **"what happens last?"** (or "which element is special?", e.g. the last balloon you pop). That choice splits the segment into two independent smaller problems.

## Definition

Let `dp[i][j]` be the best value for the segment `a[i..j]`. Typical form:

```text
dp[i][i]  = base case (often 0)
dp[i][j]  = best over k in [i, j-1] of  ( dp[i][k] + dp[k+1][j] + cost(i, j, k) )
answer    = dp[0][n-1]
```

- There are `O(n^2)` segments and `O(n)` split points each, so **`O(n^3)` time and `O(n^2)` memory**.
- The loops go by **increasing length**, never by `i` then `j`: a segment depends on strictly shorter ones, and iterating by length guarantees they are computed.
- `cost(i, j, k)` is cheap to evaluate, usually with a **prefix-sum** array for "total weight of `i..j`".

```text
for length in 2..n:
    for i in 0..n-length:
        j = i + length - 1
        dp[i][j] = best over k of ...
```

## Example, step by step

The merge-the-crates problem: weigh `4 1 1 4`; welding two neighbours costs the weight of the result.

- Single crates cost 0.
- `dp[1][2]` (the two 1s) is `0 + 0 + (1 + 1) = 2`.
- `dp[0][1]` (4 and 1) is `5`, and `dp[2][3]` is `5`.
- `dp[0][2]` (4, 1, 1, total 6): split after 0 gives `0 + dp[1][2] + 6 = 8`; split after 1 gives `dp[0][1] + 0 + 6 = 11`; best `8`.
- `dp[1][3]` (1, 1, 4, total 6): similarly `8`.
- `dp[0][3]` (total 10): split after 0: `0 + dp[1][3] + 10 = 18`; after 1: `dp[0][1] + dp[2][3] + 10 = 5 + 5 + 10 = 20`; after 2: `dp[0][2] + 0 + 10 = 18`. The minimum is **18**.

Notice how the cost of the last weld, the total weight of the segment, does not depend on `k`: only the two sub-answers do.

## The family of interval DPs

All of these have the same skeleton with a different "what happens last" and a different `cost`.

| problem | state `dp[i][j]` | the choice `k` | cost term |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| merge adjacent piles (this lesson) | cheapest way to merge `i..j` into one | where the last merge splits | total weight of `i..j` |
| matrix chain multiplication | cheapest way to multiply matrices `i..j` | where the last product splits | `d[i] * d[k+1] * d[j+1]` |
| burst balloons | most coins from balloons strictly between `i` and `j` | which balloon bursts **last** | `a[i] * a[k] * a[j]` |
| longest palindromic subsequence | longest palindrome inside `s[i..j]` | no `k`: look at the two ends | `dp[i+1][j-1] + 2` if `s[i] == s[j]`, else the max of dropping an end |
| optimal binary search tree | cheapest tree for keys `i..j` | which key is the root | total access frequency of `i..j` |
| two-player removal game | best score difference for the player to move on `i..j` | take the left or the right end | `a[i] - dp[i+1][j]` or `a[j] - dp[i][j-1]` |

Two shapes appear: "split at `k`" (the first three and the BST) and "peel an end" (the last two) which is only `O(n^2)` because there is no inner loop.

**Why "last" and not "first"?** In burst balloons, bursting a balloon changes who its neighbours are, so subproblems after "first burst" are not independent. If you decide which balloon bursts **last** in `(i, j)`, its neighbours at that moment are exactly `i` and `j`, and the two sides are independent. Choosing the right "special" element is the whole trick.

## Speeding it up

- **Knuth's optimisation:** when the cost satisfies the quadrangle inequality (true for merge cost and optimal BST), the best split point `opt[i][j]` is monotone: `opt[i][j-1] <= opt[i][j] <= opt[i+1][j]`. Restrict the inner loop to that range and the total work falls from `O(n^3)` to `O(n^2)`.
- **Divide-and-conquer optimisation** and **Hu-Tucker / Garsia-Wachs** for even better special cases.
- Use `n^2` memory carefully: `n = 5000` needs 25 million entries, so a rolling array over length sometimes helps.

## Pitfalls

1. **Wrong iteration order.** A row-by-row `for i, for j` fills `dp[i][j]` before `dp[k+1][j]`. Loop by **length** (or `i` from `n-1` down to `0`, `j` upward).
2. **Off-by-one in the split.** With `dp[i][k] + dp[k+1][j]`, `k` runs from `i` to `j-1`, so both halves are non-empty.
3. **Forgetting the base case.** Single elements usually cost 0; some problems need `dp[i][i-1] = 0` for empty segments.
4. **Overflow.** Costs multiply or sum large weights: use 64-bit integers.
5. **Choosing "first" instead of "last"** when subproblems are not independent (burst balloons).
6. **Using `float("inf")` additions blindly.** `inf + x` is fine in Python but sentinel values like `INT_MAX` overflow in C++ and Java.
7. **Circular rows.** Duplicate the array (`a + a`) and take the best window of length `n`, or fix a cut point.

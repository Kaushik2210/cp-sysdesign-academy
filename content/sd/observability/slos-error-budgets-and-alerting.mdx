## Intuition

How reliable should a service be? "As reliable as possible" sounds right and is wrong. Each extra nine of availability tends to cost far more than the last (more redundancy, slower and safer releases, more people on call), and beyond a point it is **invisible to users**, whose own phone, Wi-Fi and browser already fail more often than that. The honest question is: **how unreliable can we afford to be?**

Site reliability engineering turns that into a number, a budget and a rule:

- an **SLI** (service level indicator) measures what users experience,
- an **SLO** (service level objective) says how good that measurement must be,
- the **error budget** is the allowed shortfall, and it is **spent deliberately**: on launches, experiments and maintenance, as well as on failures.

## The vocabulary

| term | meaning | example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **SLI** | a measured ratio of **good events to valid events** | the share of checkout requests that return successfully in under 800 ms |
| **SLO** | a target for the SLI over a window | 99.9% of checkout requests are good, over 30 days |
| **SLA** | a contract with consequences (credits, penalties) if broken; usually looser than the SLO | 99.5% or the customer gets a refund |
| **error budget** | `1 - SLO`: the fraction of events allowed to be bad | 0.1% of requests per 30 days |

A good SLI is expressed as **good / valid**, so 0% to 100%, and is measured as close to the user as practical (at the load balancer or in the client), not from inside the server that may be the one that is broken. Typical SLIs: **availability** (successful requests), **latency** (requests faster than a threshold, not an average), **freshness** (data updated within N minutes), **correctness**, and **durability** for storage. Define them **per user journey** (log in, search, pay), not per microservice.

Two lessons hide in that output. At **99.9%** you may be down about **43 minutes a month**; at 99.99% about **4 minutes**, which is shorter than many failovers take. And dependencies **multiply**: a request that needs five 99.9% services cannot be better than 99.5%, so the availability you can promise is bounded by your hard dependencies. **Redundancy** helps only if failures are **independent**; two replicas in the same rack or behind the same bad config fail together.

## The error budget in practice

The budget is a shared currency between the people who want to ship and the people who want stability:

- **Budget left?** Ship, experiment, take planned risks.
- **Budget nearly gone or spent?** Slow down: freeze risky launches, fix the causes of the burn first.
- This replaces arguments about opinions with a number both sides agreed to **in advance**, written as an **error budget policy** ("if we exceed the budget, feature releases pause until the 30-day figure recovers, except for reliability and security fixes").
- Pick SLOs a little **below what the system actually achieves** and what users need; an SLO that is always 100% met means you are wasting velocity, and one that is always missed is ignored.

## Alerting on the budget: burn rate

Alerting on "error rate above 1% for 5 minutes" sounds sensible and fails in both directions: it pages on harmless blips and it **never notices a slow leak** that is quietly eating the month.

**Burn rate** is how fast you are spending the budget relative to the rate that would spend exactly all of it by the end of the window:

```text
burn rate = (observed error ratio) / (1 - SLO)

burn rate 1    -> the budget lasts exactly the whole 30 days
burn rate 14.4 -> a 30-day budget is gone in 30 / 14.4 = ~2.1 days (2% of it every hour)
burn rate 6    -> gone in 5 days (5% in 6 hours)
```

The **multi-window, multi-burn-rate** recipe from Google's SRE Workbook uses a long window for significance and a short one to stop alerting soon after recovery:

| severity | burn rate | long window | short window | budget spent in the long window |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| page | 14.4 | 1 hour | 5 minutes | 2% |
| page | 6 | 6 hours | 30 minutes | 5% |
| ticket | 1 | 3 days | 6 hours | 10% |

Both windows must exceed the burn rate. A fast, severe outage pages quickly; a moderate problem pages after hours; a slow leak opens a ticket in time to fix it during working hours.

## Simulating a month

The program below generates a month of per-minute traffic (1,000 requests a minute, SLO 99.9%) with a noisy baseline, about two short error blips a day, one **25-minute outage** at 40% errors, and a **5-day slow leak** at 0.5% errors. It then replays four alert rules over the same data and reports what each one did.

<CodeRunner language="python" timeoutMs={30000} code={`import random

random.seed(11)
MIN_PER_DAY = 1440
N = 30 * MIN_PER_DAY                          # one 30-day window, minute by minute
REQ = 1000                                    # requests per minute
SLO = 0.999
BUDGET_RATE = 1 - SLO                         # 0.1% of requests may fail

# error ratio per minute: a small baseline, brief noisy blips, one outage and one slow leak
ratio = [0.0004 + random.random() * 0.0004 for _ in range(N)]
for _ in range(60):                           # about 2 blips a day: 1 to 2 minutes at 3% errors
    start = random.randrange(N - 2)
    for m in range(start, start + random.randint(1, 2)):
        ratio[m] = 0.03
OUTAGE_START = 10 * MIN_PER_DAY + 13 * 60     # day 10, 13:00: 25 minutes with 40% of requests failing
for m in range(OUTAGE_START, OUTAGE_START + 25):
    ratio[m] = 0.40
LEAK_START = 20 * MIN_PER_DAY                 # days 20 to 25: a slow leak at 0.5% errors
LEAK_END = LEAK_START + 5 * MIN_PER_DAY
for m in range(LEAK_START, LEAK_END):
    ratio[m] = 0.005

errors = [r * REQ for r in ratio]
prefix = [0.0]
for e in errors:
    prefix.append(prefix[-1] + e)

def error_rate(t, window):                    # error ratio over the \`window\` minutes ending at minute t
    lo = max(0, t + 1 - window)
    return (prefix[t + 1] - prefix[lo]) / (REQ * (t + 1 - lo))

def burn(t, window):                          # 1.0 means "spending the budget exactly as fast as it is granted"
    return error_rate(t, window) / BUDGET_RATE

total_requests, total_errors = REQ * N, prefix[-1]
budget = BUDGET_RATE * total_requests
print(f"month: {total_requests:,} requests, {total_errors:,.0f} failed -> SLI {100 * (1 - total_errors / total_requests):.3f}% (target {100 * SLO}%)")
print(f"error budget: {budget:,.0f} failed requests; used {100 * total_errors / budget:.0f}%")
print(f"  the 25-minute outage alone used {100 * sum(errors[OUTAGE_START:OUTAGE_START + 25]) / budget:.0f}% of the budget")
print(f"  the 5-day slow leak used        {100 * sum(errors[LEAK_START:LEAK_END]) / budget:.0f}% of the budget")

WARMUP = 3 * MIN_PER_DAY                      # the 3-day window needs 3 days of data before it means anything
def evaluate(condition):
    return [t >= WARMUP and condition(t) for t in range(N)]

policies = {
    "naive: 5-minute error rate above 1%": evaluate(lambda t: error_rate(t, 5) > 0.01),
    "page: burn rate > 14.4 over 1h and over 5m": evaluate(lambda t: burn(t, 60) > 14.4 and burn(t, 5) > 14.4),
    "page: burn rate > 6 over 6h and over 30m": evaluate(lambda t: burn(t, 360) > 6 and burn(t, 30) > 6),
    "ticket: burn rate > 1 over 3d and over 6h": evaluate(lambda t: burn(t, 3 * 1440) > 1 and burn(t, 360) > 1),
}

def episodes(fires):
    """Collapse per-minute booleans into (start minute, length) episodes."""
    out, start = [], None
    for t, f in enumerate(fires + [False]):
        if f and start is None:
            start = t
        if not f and start is not None:
            out.append((start, t - start))
            start = None
    return out

print()
for name, fires in policies.items():
    eps = episodes(fires)
    outage = [e for e in eps if OUTAGE_START <= e[0] < OUTAGE_START + 25]
    leak = [e for e in eps if LEAK_START <= e[0] < LEAK_END]
    false_alarms = len(eps) - len(outage) - len(leak)          # alerts raised while nothing was really wrong
    found_outage = f"{outage[0][0] - OUTAGE_START} min after it began" if outage else "never"
    found_leak = f"{(leak[0][0] - LEAK_START) / 60:.1f} h after it began" if leak else "never"
    print(name)
    print(f"   outage: {found_outage:22s} slow leak: {found_leak:20s} alerts during blips only: {false_alarms}")
`} />

What the run shows:

- **The month failed its SLO**: the SLI ended at 99.839%, with 161% of the budget spent. The outage alone cost 23% of it, the slow leak 83%, and the rest (about 55%) was baseline errors and blips.
- The **naive 5-minute rule** caught the outage instantly but raised **24 alerts on blips alone** (a page every day or so that nobody could act on), and it **never noticed the slow leak**, because 0.5% never crosses 1%.
- The **fast burn page (14.4x)** fired **one minute** into the outage and had **no false alerts**.
- The **6x page** fired four minutes in, and **did not fire for the slow leak**: 0.5% errors is a burn rate of 5, below 6.
- The **ticket rule (1x over 3 days)** caught the slow leak **about 6 hours after it began**, with the budget still mostly intact, and made no false alerts.

That is the point of the design: **alert on how fast the budget is being spent, in proportion to how fast you must respond.**

## Making it work

- Start with **one to three SLOs** for your most important user journeys; do not publish 40.
- **Measure from the user's side**, count only valid requests (exclude load-tester and client-error traffic that is not your fault), and decide up front how to count partial failures and timeouts.
- Keep **causes on dashboards**, not pagers: CPU, queue depth and replica lag explain an alert; they should not be the alert.
- Every page must be **urgent, actionable and new**; review noisy alerts every week. Attach a **runbook** to each.
- Run **blameless postmortems** for incidents that burn significant budget, with concrete follow-up work owned and tracked.
- Revisit SLOs **quarterly**: change them when user needs or architecture changes, not to make a bad month look good.

## Pitfalls

- Aiming for 100%: it cannot be met, and it leaves no room to ship.
- Measuring only what the server believes (its own success counters) while users time out before reaching it.
- Using averages for latency SLIs instead of a threshold on a fraction of requests.
- Setting an SLO that the **dependencies** cannot support (five 99.9% services in series).
- No policy for what happens when the budget is spent, so the number changes nothing.
- Alerts that fire on every blip, until people stop trusting the pager.
- Counting scheduled maintenance, or errors caused by clients, inconsistently from month to month.
- Treating the SLA and the SLO as the same number.

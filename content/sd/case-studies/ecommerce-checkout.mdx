## 1. Clarify requirements

**Functional**
- Browse and **search** a catalog of products (variants, images, reviews, prices).
- A **cart** (guest and logged-in) that survives sessions and devices.
- **Checkout**: address, shipping options, taxes, discounts, payment, order confirmation.
- **Order management**: status, cancellation, returns, refunds, notifications.
- Sellers or warehouses update inventory and prices.

**Non-functional**
- **Browsing and cart must be highly available** (an error loses a sale); the **order and payment** step must be **correct** (no oversell, no double charge).
- Fast pages (product page p99 under about 300 ms), spiky traffic (Black Friday is 10 to 20 times normal).
- Prices, stock and promotions change frequently but need not be perfectly fresh on listing pages.
- Assume 50M daily visitors, 2M orders per day, 100M products.

## 2. Estimate

- Page views: 50M visitors × 20 pages = 1B/day ≈ **11,600/s** average, ~100,000/s at peak. Overwhelmingly reads, mostly cacheable.
- Orders: 2M/day ≈ **23/s** average; peak events 20x = ~500/s. Writes are few, but each is a multi-step transaction.
- Catalog: 100M × 5 KB = **500 GB** of structured data plus images in object storage/CDN (petabytes). Easy to shard and cache.
- Carts: 50M daily visitors × 20% creating a cart × 1 KB = ~10 GB of active cart data: small.
- Insight: **read path = caching and search at scale; write path = correctness and workflow**. Design them separately.

## 3. Service decomposition

Split along business capabilities, each owning its data (see the microservices lessons):

| Service | Owns | Consistency need |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Catalog | Products, variants, descriptions, images | Eventual; cached heavily |
| Search | Derived search index | Eventual (CQRS read model fed by catalog events) |
| Pricing and promotions | Prices, discount rules, coupons | Authoritative at checkout, cached on listings |
| Inventory | Stock levels per SKU and warehouse | **Strong at reserve time**, approximate on listings |
| Cart | Per-user line items | High availability; mergeable |
| Orders | Order records and state machine | Strong |
| Payments | Authorisation and capture (see the payments lesson) | Strong, idempotent |
| Fulfilment/shipping | Picking, packing, carrier labels, tracking | Eventual |
| Notifications, reviews, recommendations | Their own stores | Eventual |

## 4. Catalog and product pages (the read path)

- Catalog data is read-mostly: **cache aggressively** at the CDN (static assets, images, even full product pages for anonymous users) and in a look-aside cache for product JSON. Invalidate on update via events.
- **Personalised or volatile parts** (price, stock badge, delivery estimate, recommendations) are fetched separately by small API calls (or edge-side includes) so the heavy page shell stays cacheable.
- **Search and browse** use the inverted-index design from the search module: catalog events (CDC/outbox) feed the indexer; facets (brand, price range) are aggregations; ranking mixes BM25 with popularity, conversion and availability signals.
- Show **"in stock"** from a cached, approximate view. An approximate "Only 3 left" is fine; the authoritative check happens at reservation.

## 5. Cart

Requirements: always writable, merges guest and logged-in carts, tolerates concurrent edits from two devices.

- Store `cart_id → lines` in a key-value store (Redis with persistence, DynamoDB/Cassandra) keyed by user id or an anonymous cookie id. The Dynamo paper's shopping cart is the famous example: prefer **availability and merge** over strict consistency.
- Conflicts on concurrent edits: treat the cart as a set of lines with quantities and **merge** (union of items; max or latest quantity), accepting the occasional resurrected item over losing an add-to-cart.
- A cart holds **no inventory reservation** and **no price guarantee**: prices and stock are re-validated at checkout. Show a clear "price changed" message.
- Expire abandoned carts after a retention period; use them for reminder emails (respecting consent).

## 6. Checkout and price calculation

Pricing is deterministic arithmetic in **integer minor units**, computed by one authoritative service. Taxes, shipping and discounts all depend on address and cart contents, so recompute everything server-side at checkout; never trust prices from the client.

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Line:
    sku: str; unit_cents: int; qty: int

def total(lines, coupon_pct=0, free_ship_over=5000, ship=599, tax_bp=800):
    sub = sum(l.unit_cents * l.qty for l in lines)
    disc = sub * coupon_pct // 100
    after = sub - disc
    shipping = 0 if after >= free_ship_over else ship
    tax = (after * tax_bp + 5000) // 10000            # basis points, rounded half up, on the discounted amount
    return {"subtotal": sub, "discount": disc, "shipping": shipping, "tax": tax, "total": after + shipping + tax}

print(total([Line("a", 1999, 2), Line("b", 500, 1)], coupon_pct=10))
# {'subtotal': 4498, 'discount': 449, 'shipping': 599, 'tax': 324, 'total': 4972}
```

Rules worth stating: define rounding once (and who absorbs the cent), order of operations for stackable discounts, tax jurisdiction rules from a tax service, and store the **price breakdown on the order** so later refunds use exactly what was charged.

## 7. Inventory reservation and the order saga

Overselling is the cost of eventual consistency in the wrong place. At checkout, **reserve** stock atomically; **commit** it when payment succeeds; **release** it if payment fails or the reservation times out.

```python
class Stock:
    def __init__(self, n): self.n, self.reserved = n, {}
    def available(self): return self.n - sum(self.reserved.values())
    def reserve(self, order, q):
        if self.available() < q: return False        # atomic check-and-reserve (a conditional update in real life)
        self.reserved[order] = q; return True
    def commit(self, order): self.n -= self.reserved.pop(order)
    def release(self, order): self.reserved.pop(order, None)

s = Stock(3)
print(s.reserve("o1", 2), s.reserve("o2", 2), s.reserve("o3", 1))   # True False True   (o2 cannot oversell)
s.release("o1"); print(s.reserve("o2", 2))                          # True   (o1's reservation released)
s.commit("o2"); print(s.n, s.available())                           # 1 0
```

In a database this is `UPDATE stock SET reserved = reserved + :q WHERE sku = :sku AND on_hand - reserved >= :q` with a check of affected rows, plus an expiry timestamp on the reservation row and a sweeper that releases expired ones (the same hold pattern as the ticket booking lesson). For very hot SKUs (a flash sale), split stock into several buckets or use a queue per SKU to serialise decrements.

**Create-order saga (orchestrated):**

1. Create the order in state `PENDING` (idempotency key = checkout session id).
2. Reserve inventory (compensation: release).
3. Authorise payment (compensation: void the authorisation).
4. Mark the order `CONFIRMED`, emit `OrderPlaced` through the outbox.
5. Fulfilment creates the shipment; payment is **captured** when the item ships (or at confirmation for digital goods).

If any step fails, run compensations in reverse and mark the order `FAILED` with the reason shown to the user. Persist saga state after each step so a crash resumes or compensates (see the transactions lesson). Order states form a state machine: `PENDING → CONFIRMED → PACKED → SHIPPED → DELIVERED`, with `CANCELLED` and `RETURNED` branches; only valid transitions are accepted.

## 8. Peak traffic and operational concerns

- **Capacity and load shedding:** pre-scale before known events (Black Friday); serve a **degraded mode** (static pages, disable recommendations and reviews, queue non-critical work) rather than failing; protect checkout with bulkheads and a waiting room if inventory is scarce (as in the ticketing lesson).
- **Idempotent checkout:** a user double-clicks or the browser retries after a timeout: one idempotency key per checkout session means one order.
- **Promotions and flash deals:** precompute and cache eligible prices; limit coupon redemptions with an atomic counter; protect against coupon abuse and bots.
- **Returns and refunds:** a return is its own workflow referencing the original order lines; refunds go back through payments with the original price breakdown; restock only after inspection.
- **Multi-warehouse and delivery promise:** choose the fulfilment node by stock, distance and cost; compute delivery estimates from carrier and warehouse cut-off times; split shipments when needed.
- **Data consistency checks:** reconcile orders vs payments vs shipments daily to find stuck sagas, charged-but-unshipped orders and stock drift; fix automatically where safe.
- **Observability:** conversion funnel (view → cart → checkout → paid), checkout latency and error rate by step, payment decline rate, oversell/stock-adjustment counts, saga stuck age, search zero-result rate.
- **Security and privacy:** PCI scope isolation (tokenised cards), fraud scoring on orders, account takeover defences, address and PII protection (see the security module).

## Trade-offs to state aloud

- Availability on browsing and carts vs correctness on inventory and payment: **different consistency per capability**, not one choice for the whole system.
- Reserve at add-to-cart (feels fair, locks stock for abandoners) vs reserve at checkout (higher availability, a small chance of "sorry, sold out" late); most retailers reserve at checkout, and ticketing reserves at selection.
- Monolithic order transaction vs saga: a single database transaction is simpler but couples teams; the saga scales organisationally at the cost of compensations and intermediate states.
- Fresh price everywhere vs cached listing price with recheck at checkout.

## Pitfalls

- Trusting client-supplied prices or totals.
- Reserving inventory at add-to-cart with no expiry.
- Decrementing stock after payment (oversell window) or before payment with no release path.
- Floats for money and inconsistent rounding between cart, order and refund.
- Strong consistency on the product page path, which makes browsing fragile.
- No idempotency on order creation.
- Hard-coding one consistency model for all services.

## What would you choose and why?

A marketplace lets thousands of third-party sellers update stock through APIs and CSV uploads, often with stale data.

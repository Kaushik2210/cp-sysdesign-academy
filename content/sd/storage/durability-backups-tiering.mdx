## Intuition

"Don't lose data" sounds like one requirement but is really four:

1. **Durability**: stored data survives hardware failure.
2. **Availability**: data can be read right now.
3. **Recoverability**: if someone (or a bug) destroys or corrupts data, you can get it back.
4. **Cost**: all of the above at a price the business accepts.

Durability is solved by replication and erasure coding. It does *not* solve human error: replication faithfully copies a `DELETE` or a corrupt write to every replica in milliseconds. That is what backups, versioning and immutability are for.

## Formal definitions

- **Durability**: probability data is not lost over a period. Quoted in nines: 11 nines per object per year means the expected loss is about one object in 100 billion per year (a design target).
- **RPO** (recovery point objective): how much recent data you can afford to lose, measured in time. Hourly backups mean RPO is up to one hour.
- **RTO** (recovery time objective): how long recovery may take before the business is unacceptably hurt.
- **Replication vs backup**: replication protects against *failure*; backup protects against *change* (deletion, corruption, ransomware, bad deploy).

| Mechanism | Protects against | Does not protect against |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Replication within a region | Disk, node, rack, zone failure | Accidental delete, corruption, malicious change |
| Cross-region replication | Regional outage | Delete or corruption (it replicates too) |
| Snapshots / versioning | Accidental overwrite or delete | Loss of the storage account itself |
| Offline or immutable backup (object lock, separate account) | Ransomware, insider threat, account compromise | Slow recovery if not rehearsed |

A practical baseline is the **3-2-1 rule**: three copies, on two different media or systems, one off-site or in a separate administrative domain.

## Tiered storage and lifecycle

Most data is hot briefly and cold forever: photos are viewed a lot in week one and almost never after a year. Providers therefore offer tiers that trade retrieval speed and fees for cheaper storage:

| Tier (S3 names as an example) | Storage price | Retrieval | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Standard | Highest | Milliseconds, no retrieval fee | Active data |
| Infrequent access | Lower | Milliseconds, per-GB retrieval fee, minimum storage duration | Monthly access, backups |
| Archive (Glacier-style) | Much lower | Minutes to hours, retrieval fee | Compliance, old backups |
| Deep archive | Lowest | Hours (around half a day) | Rarely read, long retention |

Prices vary by region and change over time; as a rough shape, archive tiers can be an order of magnitude cheaper per GB-month than standard, with retrieval fees, minimum retention periods and per-request charges as the catch. Intelligent-tiering features move objects automatically based on access, for a small monitoring fee per object.

A **lifecycle rule** automates it, for example: after 30 days move to infrequent access, after 180 days to archive, after 7 years delete, and abort incomplete multipart uploads after 7 days.

## Worked example: monthly cost of 500 TB of logs

Assume (approximate, illustrative prices) standard storage at about $0.023 per GB-month, infrequent access at about $0.0125, and archive at about $0.004. For 500 TB = 500,000 GB:

- All standard: 500,000 x 0.023 = $11,500 per month.
- 10% hot, 30% infrequent, 60% archive: 50,000 x 0.023 + 150,000 x 0.0125 + 300,000 x 0.004 = 1,150 + 1,875 + 1,200 = $4,225 per month.

Savings are about 63%, before retrieval and request charges. The decision hinges on how often the cold data is actually read: if archive data is restored every week the retrieval fees can erase the saving.

Also remember **egress**: moving data out of a cloud region or to the internet is typically billed per GB (on the order of several cents), which is why architectures keep compute near the data.

## Failure modes to discuss

- **Untested restore.** A backup you have never restored is a hope, not a backup. Schedule restore drills and measure real RTO.
- **Silent corruption.** Use checksums end to end and periodic scrubbing; replicas can silently diverge.
- **Backups in the same blast radius.** If the same credentials can delete production and backups, one compromise removes both.
- **Retention conflicts.** Legal hold or compliance requires keeping; privacy law requires deleting. Design per-data-class retention.

## Pitfalls

- Equating "11 nines durability" with "never lose data" or with availability.
- Tiering tiny objects: per-object overhead and minimum billable sizes can make archive more expensive.
- Forgetting to expire old object versions, so storage silently doubles.
- Ignoring request and retrieval costs in the model.

## What would you choose and why?

A healthcare startup stores 200 TB of scan images. Regulations require seven-year retention, doctors need images from the last 90 days instantly, and older scans are fetched a few times a day with a tolerable delay of minutes. Define RPO and RTO, a tiering plan, and the protection against ransomware. Which of your choices would you revisit if retrieval fees turned out to dominate the bill?

## The problem

Design a **ticket booking system**: users browse events (concerts, movies, flights, trains), see which seats are free, pick seats, pay, and receive tickets. The same design covers **hotel rooms, appointment slots and flash-sale products**: anything where a **limited number of unique items** is sold and **two people must never get the same one**.

Nothing here is computationally heavy. The challenge is **contention and correctness**. When a popular concert goes on sale, hundreds of thousands of people want the same few thousand seats at the same instant. The system must **never sell a seat twice**, must **hold a seat fairly while someone pays**, must **survive a stampede**, and must **handle payment and failure cases cleanly**.

## 1. Requirements

**Functional**

- Browse events and **view the seat map with availability**.
- **Select seats and hold them** for a short time while the user pays.
- **Pay** and receive **tickets** (QR codes) by email and in the app.
- **Cancel and refund** within rules; waitlists are an extension.

**Non-functional**

- **No overselling, ever**: each seat goes to exactly one buyer.
- **Fairness and stability under a spike**: a huge crowd arrives within seconds when sales open.
- **Low latency** browsing (reads outnumber writes enormously) and acceptable booking latency.
- **High availability** for browsing; **strong consistency** for the inventory decision.
- **Abuse resistance**: bots and scalpers.

## 2. Estimation

A typical day is quiet, but an **on-sale event** is a spike. Assume 500,000 users arrive within 10 seconds for 50,000 seats, each user looking at the seat map about 10 times before choosing.

<CodeRunner language="python" code={`users, seats, window = 500_000, 50_000, 10
views_per_user = 10
map_reads = users * views_per_user
print(f"offered load at the moment of sale: {users / window:,.0f} users per second hitting the site")
print(f"seat map reads: {map_reads / 1e6:.0f} million in the first minute or so; actual purchases: at most {seats:,}")
print(f"read to write ratio: {map_reads / seats:,.0f} : 1")
print(f"only {seats / users:.0%} of the people who show up can get a seat, so about {1 - seats / users:.0%} will be disappointed however well we do")
`} />

Read traffic dwarfs writes (about 100 reads per seat sold), the load is **instantaneous** (not ramping), and **90% of the crowd cannot succeed**. That last fact shapes the design: much of the system's job is to **say "no" or "wait" cheaply and fairly**.

## 3. Data model

```text
events      (event_id, name, venue_id, starts_at, on_sale_at, ...)
seats       (event_id, seat_id, section, row, number, price, status, held_by, hold_expires_at, order_id)
              status: available | held | sold
orders      (order_id, user_id, event_id, status, total, payment_id, created_at)
              status: pending | paid | cancelled | refunded
tickets     (ticket_id, order_id, event_id, seat_id, qr_token)  -- UNIQUE (event_id, seat_id)
```

For **general admission** (no assigned seats) replace per-seat rows with a counter per ticket type, decremented atomically. The principles are the same.

Use a **relational database** for inventory and orders: you want transactions, constraints and conditional updates. Shard by `event_id` when you must: all contention for one event then lands on one shard, which is also the unit you can scale up for a big on-sale.

## 4. The double-booking race

The naive purchase flow is: read the seat, **check in the application** that it is free, then write "sold to me". Between the read and the write, someone else can do the same. This is a **check-then-act race** (a lost update, see [ACID and transactions](/sd/databases-1/acid-and-transactions/)).

The program simulates 300 buyers, each picking a random seat out of 100, with their read and write happening at random moments so the steps of different buyers interleave. It compares the naive approach with **one atomic conditional update** that checks and claims in a single statement.

<CodeRunner language="python" code={`import random, sqlite3

def run_naive(buyers, seats, seed):
    """Each buyer reads 'is the seat free?', then later writes 'it is mine'. Steps from different buyers interleave."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    owners = {s: [] for s in range(seats)}                  # who believes they bought each seat
    taken = set()                                           # what the database says is taken
    events = []
    for b in range(buyers):
        seat = rng.randrange(seats)
        t_read, t_write = sorted((rng.random(), rng.random()))      # each buyer reads first, then writes, at random moments
        events += [(t_read, b, 0, seat), (t_write, b, 1, seat)]
    events.sort()                                                  # the interleaving the database sees
    seen_free = {}
    for _, b, step, seat in events:
        if step == 0:
            seen_free[b] = seat not in taken                # read
        elif seen_free.get(b):
            owners[seat].append(b)                          # write, based on a read that may be stale
            taken.add(seat)
    sold = sum(len(v) for v in owners.values())
    double = sum(1 for v in owners.values() if len(v) > 1)
    return sold, double

def run_atomic(buyers, seats, seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
    db.execute("CREATE TABLE seats(id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, buyer INTEGER)")
    db.executemany("INSERT INTO seats(id) VALUES (?)", [(s,) for s in range(seats)])
    owners = {s: [] for s in range(seats)}                  # buyers who were told "the seat is yours"
    for b in range(buyers):
        seat = rng.randrange(seats)
        cur = db.execute("UPDATE seats SET buyer = ? WHERE id = ? AND buyer IS NULL", (b, seat))   # check and claim in ONE statement
        if cur.rowcount == 1:
            owners[seat].append(b)
    sold = sum(len(v) for v in owners.values())
    double = sum(1 for v in owners.values() if len(v) > 1)
    return sold, double

print("300 buyers each try to take one random seat out of 100 at the same time:")
n_sold, n_double = run_naive(300, 100, seed=2)
a_sold, a_double = run_atomic(300, 100, seed=2)
print(f"  check in the app, then write   : {n_sold} purchases recorded for 100 seats, {n_double} seats sold to more than one buyer")
print(f"  one atomic conditional UPDATE  : {a_sold} purchases recorded, {a_double} seats sold twice")
`} />

The naive flow recorded **193 purchases for 100 seats**, with **60 seats sold to more than one buyer**. The atomic version recorded **95 purchases** (some seats were never picked in this random run, and a real buyer who lost a seat would pick another), and **no seat was sold twice**.

The options for enforcing "one buyer per seat", from best to worst for this problem:

| approach | how it works | notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **conditional update** | `UPDATE seats SET status='held', held_by=:u WHERE seat_id=:s AND status='available'`; if 0 rows changed, someone else won | one statement, no explicit lock, the database serialises writers; the default choice |
| **unique constraint** | `UNIQUE (event_id, seat_id)` on tickets; the second insert fails | a safety net even if application logic has a bug |
| **pessimistic lock** | `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE` locks the row until commit (`SKIP LOCKED` helps pick any free seat) | correct, but holds locks across round trips; keep the transaction tiny |
| **optimistic version check** | `UPDATE ... WHERE version = :seen`; retry on conflict | good when conflicts are rare; at an on-sale conflicts are constant |
| **distributed lock (Redis `SET NX` with TTL)** | take a lock per seat in a cache | fast, but locks can expire or be lost (see Kleppmann's analysis); never the only protection, keep the database constraint as the source of truth |

**Never rely on the application to check and the database to trust it.** The database must enforce the invariant.

## 5. Holding seats while the user pays

Payment takes a minute or more. If seats are sold only at payment time, a user can pick seats, type their card number, and discover the seats went to someone else. The standard solution is a **hold**: when the user selects seats they become **`held` with an expiry** (for example 5 minutes). Other buyers cannot take them; if the user does not pay in time, the hold expires and the seats return to the pool.

State machine for a seat: `available` → `held` (with `held_by` and `hold_expires_at`) → `sold`, and `held` → `available` on expiry or release.

- **Holding is the same atomic conditional update** as before, plus the expiry timestamp.
- **Expiry should not depend on a background job being on time.** Treat a hold as expired if `hold_expires_at < now()` **when you read or claim it** (lazy expiry); a sweeper job then tidies up and publishes availability changes. Use the **database clock**, not application clocks.
- **Confirming the sale** must re-check the hold in one statement: `UPDATE seats SET status='sold', order_id=:o WHERE seat_id=:s AND status='held' AND held_by=:u AND hold_expires_at > now()`. If it changes zero rows, the hold lapsed before payment completed.
- The tricky case: **payment succeeds after the hold expired and someone else took the seat.** The order cannot be completed, so the system **refunds** (a compensating action, see [sagas](/sd/distributed-theory/distributed-commit-and-sagas/)) or, better, **extends the hold when payment starts** and keeps a short grace period so the common case never happens. All payment calls use idempotency keys ([payment system](/sd/case-studies-core-services/design-a-payment-system/)).

**How long should a hold last?** A longer hold is kinder to slow payers but blocks seats that abandoners never intend to buy. The simulation sells 1,000 seats to 40 buyers arriving each minute, where **half of them abandon** after choosing a seat, with three hold durations.

<CodeRunner language="python" code={`import heapq, random

def sale(hold_minutes, seed=4, seats=1000, arrivals_per_min=40, minutes=400, abandon=0.5):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    free, held, sold = seats, [], 0                         # held: heap of hold-expiry times for seats nobody completed
    falsely_sold_out, peak_held, sold_out_at, t = 0, 0, None, 0.0
    while t < minutes and sold_out_at is None:
        t += rng.expovariate(arrivals_per_min)
        while held and held[0] <= t:                        # expired holds return their seats to the pool
            heapq.heappop(held)
            free += 1
        if free == 0:
            if held:                                        # no seat is actually sold out: they are only held by people who may leave
                falsely_sold_out += 1
            continue
        free -= 1                                           # the buyer holds a seat while paying
        if rng.random() < abandon:
            heapq.heappush(held, t + hold_minutes)          # abandoned: the seat stays blocked until the hold expires
            peak_held = max(peak_held, len(held))
        else:
            sold += 1
            if sold == seats:
                sold_out_at = t
    return sold_out_at, falsely_sold_out, peak_held

print("selling 1,000 seats, 40 buyers arrive a minute, half of them abandon after choosing a seat:")
for hold in (2, 5, 15):
    done, false_sold_out, peak = sale(hold)
    print(f"  hold of {hold:2d} min: sold out after {done:6.1f} min, {false_sold_out:5,d} buyers were told 'sold out' while seats were only held, up to {peak} seats blocked at once")
`} />

The trade-off is clear:

- With a **2-minute hold** the event sold out after about **60 minutes**, and only 400 buyers saw a false "sold out".
- With a **5-minute hold**: about **79 minutes**, 1,207 false "sold out" messages, up to 116 seats blocked at once.
- With a **15-minute hold**: about **195 minutes** (more than three times as long), **5,692 buyers** told "sold out" for seats that were merely held, and up to **310 seats blocked** by abandoners.

So hold time is a **product knob**: long enough for a normal payment (a few minutes, shown with a visible countdown), short enough that abandoners do not strangle the sale. Good systems **release a hold early** when the user leaves the page or clicks back, and show **"seats are being held by other buyers"** instead of a flat "sold out" so people keep waiting for returns.

## 6. The stampede: waiting rooms and load control

When 500,000 people arrive at once, even a perfectly correct booking service melts if it tries to serve them all. Autoscaling cannot react in seconds (cold starts, warm-up), and retries from failing clients make overload worse (see [load shedding](/sd/scalability/load-shedding-and-bulkheads/)). The industry answer is a **virtual waiting room**: before the on-sale, people enter a **queue** served from a cheap, static, CDN-friendly layer; the queue **admits users to the real booking flow at a rate the backend can sustain**, in a fair order (first come first served, or randomised within the opening window to defeat people who just have the fastest connection).

<CodeRunner language="python" code={`def waiting_room(users, backend_rps, admit_rps=None, window=10):
    if admit_rps is None:
        peak = users / window                              # everyone arrives within \`window\` seconds and hits the backend
        return peak, min(1.0, backend_rps / peak), None
    waits = [i / admit_rps for i in range(users)]          # users are admitted at a fixed rate
    return admit_rps, 1.0, waits

users, backend = 500_000, 5_000
peak, share, _ = waiting_room(users, backend)
print(f"{users:,} users arrive within 10 s at a booking service that can do {backend:,} requests/s:")
print(f"  without a queue: {peak:,.0f} requests/s offered, only {share:.0%} can be served; the rest time out or error and retry (making it worse)")
rate, share, waits = waiting_room(users, backend, admit_rps=4_000)
print(f"  waiting room admitting {rate:,} users/s: load stays at {rate:,}/s, everyone is served, the last user waits {waits[-1] / 60:.1f} minutes (median {waits[len(waits) // 2] / 60:.1f} min)")
seats = 50_000
print(f"  with only {seats:,} seats, the first {seats:,} admitted users are enough: {seats / rate:.1f} s of admission, so the room can tell the rest 'sold out' early")
`} />

Without a queue the service is offered **50,000 requests a second** against a capacity of 5,000: **only 10% are served** and the retries pile on. With a waiting room admitting **4,000 users a second**, the load is flat and well within capacity, everyone gets a definite answer, and the last of 500,000 people waits about **2.1 minutes** (median about 1 minute). Because there are only 50,000 seats, the first admissions would sell everything in about **12.5 seconds** of admission, so the room can also tell everyone behind that point honestly "sold out" **early**, instead of making them wait to find out.

How the waiting room works in practice:

- It is a **separate, highly scalable layer** (static pages on a CDN plus a small queue service), so it survives the spike even if the booking system does not.
- Each visitor gets a **signed queue token** with a position; when their turn comes, a **time-limited admission token** lets them into the booking flow. The booking API **rejects requests without a valid admission token**, so bypassing the queue does not work.
- Admission rate is **controlled by the backend's health** (adaptive), not a fixed number.
- **Fairness and abuse**: limit tickets per account, rate limit per IP and device, require **verified accounts** or CAPTCHAs, detect bots, and consider **lottery-style access** (a random draw among everyone who registered before opening) to remove the advantage of speed and scripts.

- **Seat map reads** are served from **cached snapshots** (a few seconds old) and updated by **push** (server-sent events or WebSocket) as seats change state. Slight staleness is fine because the **hold step is the authority**: if the seat was taken, the user is told immediately and picks another.
- **Booking service** performs the atomic holds, confirmations and releases against the **inventory database**, which is **sharded by event** so a hot event is isolated; within an event, seats can be partitioned by section for parallelism.
- **The outbox pattern** ([payment system](/sd/case-studies-core-services/design-a-payment-system/)) publishes "seat sold" and "order paid" events reliably, driving **ticket issuance** and **notifications**.
- **Tickets**: issue a signed QR token per ticket and **validate at the gate** (online lookup, or an offline-verifiable signature plus a used-ticket list) to prevent duplicates and re-entry.

## 8. Failure cases to design for

- **Crash after payment, before the ticket is issued**: the order is `paid` in the database, so a reconciliation job and the event stream finish issuing tickets; it must be idempotent.
- **User double-clicks or the client retries**: every mutating request carries an **idempotency key**, so the same click does not hold or buy twice.
- **Hold sweeper is down**: lazy expiry at read time means holds still lapse correctly.
- **Database failover mid-booking**: the conditional updates and unique constraints keep the invariant; clients retry safely.
- **Payment provider slow or down**: extend holds a little while a payment is in flight, and degrade gracefully ("we are still processing your payment").
- **Cancellations and refunds**: seats return to inventory only after the refund transaction is recorded; a cancellation is a new state transition, never a deletion.
- **Bulk purchases and groups**: holding several seats must be **all or nothing** (one transaction or a rollback of partial holds), or a user ends up holding 3 of the 4 seats they wanted.

## Pitfalls

- Selling seats at payment time with no hold, so users lose seats after typing their card.
- Read-then-write availability checks in application code.
- Relying on a cache or a distributed lock as the only guard against double sales.
- Application-clock-based expiry that drifts, instead of database time and lazy checks.
- Serving seat maps from the primary database during an on-sale.
- No waiting room: letting the stampede hit the booking service directly.
- Admission tokens that are not verified by the booking API.
- Partial group holds (3 of 4 seats) with no rollback.
- Non-idempotent hold and payment calls that duplicate on retry.
- No limits per account, device or payment card, which invites scalpers.
- Forgetting to reconcile paid orders that never got tickets.

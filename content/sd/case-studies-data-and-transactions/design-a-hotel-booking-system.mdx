## The problem

Design a **hotel booking system** like Booking.com or Expedia: guests **search** for rooms in a city for a range of dates, **book** a room type, and may **cancel**. Hotels manage their rooms and rates.

It resembles the [ticket booking case study](/sd/case-studies-data-and-transactions/design-a-ticket-booking-system/), but the **unit being sold is different**, and that changes the design:

- A ticket booking sells **one seat for one event**. A hotel booking sells **one room for a range of nights**: all nights or none.
- Rooms of one type are **interchangeable**, so we usually track **counts**, not individual rooms.
- Hotels **deliberately overbook** because some guests do not show up. Rejecting every booking at 100% is not the goal.
- **Search is huge and read-heavy**; **booking is small and must be exactly right**.

## Requirements

**Functional**
- Search hotels by city, dates, guests and filters; see price and availability.
- Book a room type for a **check-in to check-out** range; get a confirmation.
- Cancel (with the hotel's policy) so the room becomes available again.
- Hotels update their inventory and prices.

**Non-functional**
- **Never sell a night that is not available** (beyond a deliberate overbooking limit). Correctness of booking beats everything else.
- **Search** can be slightly stale but must be fast (hundreds of milliseconds).
- A booking must be **all or nothing** across its nights.
- **Idempotent** booking: a retried request must not book twice.

## Estimation

| quantity | assumption | result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bookings | 1.5 million per day | about 17 per second on average |
| Peak | 10 times average | about 170 per second |
| Searches | 1,000 per booking | about 17,000 per second on average, 170,000 at peak |
| Inventory rows | 500,000 hotels x 5 room types x 365 nights | about 912 million rows |

The write rate is **modest**: one well-tuned relational database shard handles hundreds of booking transactions per second. The challenges are **correctness under concurrency**, the **huge read load** for search and the **size** of the inventory table, not raw write throughput. That points to a relational database for bookings and a separate, scalable read path for search.

## Data model: inventory per room type per night

Instead of tracking each physical room, keep a row per **(hotel, room type, night)** with the **total** rooms and how many are **booked**:

| hotel | room type | night | total | booked |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 17 | double | 2027-03-01 | 40 | 37 |
| 17 | double | 2027-03-02 | 40 | 40 |

A stay from check-in to check-out uses **every night from check-in up to, but not including, the check-out day** (a guest leaving on the 4th does not occupy the night of the 4th, so someone else can arrive that day). Assigning a physical room number can wait until check-in.

Why counts rather than rooms? The question "is there at least one double room free on each night of my stay?" is a simple comparison per night. Tracking each room would require finding a **single room free for the whole range**, which is a harder interval-packing problem and unnecessary when rooms of a type are interchangeable.

Tables: `hotel`, `room_type`, `inventory(hotel, room_type, night, total, booked)` with a **constraint `booked <= total`**, and `booking(id, hotel, room_type, check_in, check_out, guest, status, idempotency_key)`.

## The booking transaction

For every night of the stay, run an **atomic conditional update**, and make the whole thing one transaction:

```sql
BEGIN;
UPDATE inventory SET booked = booked + 1
 WHERE hotel = ? AND room_type = ? AND night = ? AND booked < total;   -- once per night
-- if any update changed 0 rows: ROLLBACK and report "sold out on that night"
INSERT INTO booking (...) VALUES (...);
COMMIT;
```

- The **`WHERE booked < total`** makes **check and claim one atomic step**: two concurrent requests for the last room cannot both succeed, because the database serialises updates to the same row and the second sees `booked = total`. (This is the same idea as the ticket booking lesson's conditional update, applied per night.)
- The **transaction** gives **all or nothing**: if night 3 of 4 is sold out, the claim already made on night 1 and 2 is rolled back.
- A **`CHECK (booked <= total)`** constraint is a **safety net**: even if a bug skips the `WHERE` clause, the database refuses to oversell.
- **Update nights in a consistent order** (ascending date) so two overlapping stays cannot deadlock by locking the same rows in opposite order.

## What the output shows

- A hotel with **2 rooms**: Ann books March 1 to 4 and Bob books March 2 to 5. Free rooms per night end up as **[1, 0, 0, 1, 2, 2, 2]**: both rooms are taken on the 2nd and 3rd, one on the 1st and on the 4th.
- **Cat wants March 2 to 4**: rejected with "sold out on 2027-03-02". **Eve wants March 1 to 4**: night 1 has a free room, so her first update succeeds, but night 2 is sold out, so the transaction **rolls back**. March 1 still shows **1 free before and after**: no partial hold is left behind.
- **Dan books March 4 to 6** and it succeeds: Ann checked out on the 4th, so she does not occupy the night of the 4th, and only Bob holds a room that night (one of two). A booking that **starts on the day another one ends** is the normal back-to-back case that off-by-one errors break.
- **Cancelling** Ann's booking returns March 1 to **2 free**.
- **The race**: when two requests both read "1 free" and then both write (the interleaving is replayed step by step here, not run on threads), a table **without** a constraint ends with **2 booked in a hotel of capacity 1**. With the **`CHECK (booked <= total)`** constraint the second write is rejected by the database. This is why the constraint exists even though the conditional update should already prevent the race.

## Overbooking as a business decision

Some guests never show up. A hotel that never overbooks has **empty rooms**; one that overbooks too much **turns guests away** (and pays to relocate them). The simulation sells 100, 104 and 108 bookings for 100 rooms, assuming **92% of guests show up**, over 10,000 nights:

| bookings sold for 100 rooms | average empty rooms | average guests turned away |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | 7.99 | 0.00 |
| 104 | 4.39 | 0.04 |
| 108 | 1.44 | 0.82 |

Selling 104 **recovers about 3.6 rooms per night** while turning away a guest on only about **1 night in 25** (0.04 per night). Selling 108 **fills the hotel better but turns away almost one guest every night on average**. The right number depends on the **cost of an empty room versus the cost of relocating a guest**, and on the **real show-up rate**, which varies by hotel, season and booking channel. Implement it as a **configurable limit** (`total` is the physical rooms; the sellable limit is `total x (1 + overbooking factor)`), never by editing the room count. The numbers come from one seeded simulation with a fixed 92% show-up rate.

## Search

Search must **not** query the booking database for every request: 170,000 searches per second would overwhelm it. Instead:

- Maintain a **search index** (for example Elasticsearch, see [search systems](/sd/search-systems/inverted-indexes-and-text-analysis/)) of hotels with their attributes, location and a **summary of availability and lowest price per date**, updated from the inventory database through **change events** ([change data capture](/sd/queues-and-streaming/kafka-rabbitmq-sqs/) into a queue).
- The index is **eventually consistent**: a search result may show a room that was just booked. That is acceptable **because the booking transaction re-checks the real inventory**. The user sees "sorry, that room just sold out" rarely, which is better than making search slow or fragile.
- **Cache** popular searches (city, dates) for seconds to minutes ([caching strategies](/sd/caching/caching-strategies/)).

## Holds, payment and idempotency

Payment takes time. Two common patterns:

1. **Book, then pay**: create the booking as `pending` (inventory is claimed) with an **expiry** (for example 10 minutes); a payment success event marks it `confirmed`; a **sweeper job** releases expired pending bookings (decrementing `booked`). This is the seat hold pattern from the ticket booking lesson.
2. **Pay on arrival**: confirm immediately, charge later; simpler but needs a no-show policy.

**Idempotency**: the client sends an **idempotency key** with the booking request and the `booking` table has a **unique constraint** on it. A retry after a timeout returns the existing booking instead of booking again ([delivery semantics and idempotency](/sd/queues-and-streaming/delivery-semantics-and-idempotency/)). Payment calls use their own idempotency keys; if payment succeeds but the confirmation write fails, a **reconciliation** job repairs the state.

## Scaling the booking database

- **Shard by hotel id**: every booking touches exactly one hotel, so a booking transaction stays on **one shard** with no distributed transaction ([sharding and partitioning](/sd/databases-2/sharding-and-partitioning/)). Hot hotels (a big event in one city) are the skew risk; cache and queue in front, and give very hot inventory its own shard.
- **Replicas** serve reporting and the guest's "my bookings" view; the booking path reads and writes the **primary** of its shard.
- **Multi-region**: a hotel's inventory has **one home region** that accepts its bookings, so two regions never sell the same last room. Other regions read replicas and forward writes.

## Pitfalls

- **Check-then-act** availability checks without an atomic update or lock.
- Treating the **check-out day** as a night stayed (or forgetting the first night): off-by-one errors in date ranges.
- Updating nights in **different orders** in different requests, causing deadlocks.
- Booking nights in separate transactions, leaving **partial holds** after a failure.
- No **constraint** (`booked <= total`) as a last line of defence.
- No **idempotency key**, so a retry double-books and double-charges.
- **Pending bookings that never expire**, slowly blocking inventory.
- **Time zones**: nights are dates in the hotel's local time zone, not the guest's.
- Editing `total` to implement overbooking instead of a separate sellable limit.
- Sending every search to the booking database.

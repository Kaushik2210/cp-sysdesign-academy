## The problem

Design the **checkout and inventory** parts of an online store like Amazon or Shopify: shoppers **browse** a catalogue, fill a **cart**, **check out**, pay, and the warehouse ships. The hard parts are not the product pages; they are the moments where **money and stock change hands**:

- Never **sell stock you do not have**, even in a flash sale with thousands of buyers for 100 items.
- Never **charge twice**, and never charge without creating an order.
- A checkout touches **several services** (inventory, payment, order, shipping), and any step can fail, so we need a plan for **partial failure**.
- The **price the customer saw** must be the price on the order, even if the catalogue price changes a second later.

## Requirements

**Functional**
- Browse and search products; view price and availability.
- Add to a cart (persisting across sessions and devices).
- Check out: reserve stock, pay, create an order, send a confirmation.
- Cancel an order before it ships; refunds on cancellation.
- Order history and status.

**Non-functional**
- **No overselling**; **exactly-once effect** for charging and ordering (despite retries).
- Browsing is **highly available and fast**; checkout is **correct first**.
- Scale to **flash sales**: for example 100x normal traffic on a few products.

## Estimation

| quantity | assumption | result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Orders | 5 million per day | about 58 per second on average |
| Peak (sales events) | 20 times average | about 1,200 orders per second |
| Product page views | 100 per order | about 5,800 per second on average, 116,000 at peak |
| Flash sale | 100 units, 400,000 shoppers in a minute | about 6,700 attempts per second on one product row |

Most traffic is **reads** that a CDN and caches absorb. The writes that matter, **inventory decrements**, can concentrate on a **single hot row** in a flash sale, which is why that path needs special design.

## Service decomposition

Each service owns its data ([service boundaries](/sd/microservice-patterns/service-boundaries-and-availability/)):

| service | owns | notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Catalogue** | product details, images, descriptions | read-heavy; cached and served from a CDN; search index alongside |
| **Cart** | per-user list of items | key-value store (Redis or a document store); losing a cart is annoying, not fatal |
| **Inventory** | stock per SKU: on hand and reserved | the strongly consistent core |
| **Order** | orders, lines and status | the system of record for what was bought and at what price |
| **Payment** | charges and refunds via a payment provider | idempotent calls ([payment system lesson](/sd/case-studies-core-services/design-a-payment-system/)) |
| **Fulfilment** | picking, packing, shipping | event-driven, asynchronous |

## Reserve, then pay

The key design move is a **reservation**. Stock has two numbers per SKU:

- **on hand**: physically in the warehouse,
- **reserved**: promised to pending orders.

**Available = on hand minus reserved.** Checkout first **reserves** stock with one atomic conditional update (`WHERE on_hand - reserved >= qty`), then charges, and only on payment success **commits** (stock leaves: both counters drop). On failure or timeout the reservation is **released**.

Why not just decrement stock when the order is placed, then charge? Because payment can fail or time out and we would have to put stock back; and why not charge first? Because we might take money for something already sold out. Reserving first means the **only possible failure at the expensive step is a payment failure**, which is handled by the **compensating release**. Reservations also need an **expiry**: a shopper who abandons the payment page must not hold stock forever.

## Distributed transactions: the saga

There is no single database across the inventory, order and payment services, and a two-phase commit across a third-party payment provider is not possible. So we use a **saga**: a sequence of **local transactions**, each with a **compensating action** that undoes its business effect ([sagas](/sd/distributed-theory/distributed-commit-and-sagas/)).

- **Orchestration**: the order service drives the steps and the compensations (clear control flow, easier to reason about; what we show).
- **Choreography**: services react to each other's events (looser coupling, harder to follow).

Every step must be **idempotent** and the saga must be **recoverable**: store the order's state in the database after each step, so a crashed orchestrator can resume (or compensate) when it restarts. Late or duplicate callbacks (a payment confirmation delivered twice) must be **ignored** once the order has left the state they apply to.

## The order state machine

`PENDING` (stock reserved, awaiting payment) to `PAID` to `SHIPPED` to `DELIVERED`, with `CANCELLED` reachable from `PENDING` and `PAID` (before shipping, with a refund). Allowed transitions are checked in code, and the update is conditional (`WHERE status = 'PENDING'`) so two racing callbacks cannot both transition the order.

## The simulation

The program uses SQLite to run the design: stock with `reserved` and `on_hand`, the reservation, an idempotent checkout, payment success and failure, an expiry sweeper, price snapshots, and finally a **flash sale race** comparing read-then-write with a conditional update. (It runs in the browser; SQLite loads on demand when you press Run.)

<CodeRunner language="python" timeoutMs={60000} code={`import random
import sqlite3

db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
db.isolation_level = None            # we issue BEGIN, COMMIT and ROLLBACK ourselves
db.executescript("""
CREATE TABLE product (sku TEXT PRIMARY KEY, price_cents INTEGER, on_hand INTEGER, reserved INTEGER DEFAULT 0,
                      CHECK (reserved >= 0 AND reserved <= on_hand));
CREATE TABLE orders (id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, idem_key TEXT UNIQUE, status TEXT, total_cents INTEGER, expires_at INTEGER);
CREATE TABLE order_item (order_id INTEGER, sku TEXT, qty INTEGER, price_cents INTEGER);
""")


def add_product(sku, price, stock):
    db.execute("INSERT INTO product (sku, price_cents, on_hand) VALUES (?, ?, ?)", (sku, price, stock))


def available(sku):
    on_hand, reserved = db.execute("SELECT on_hand, reserved FROM product WHERE sku = ?", (sku,)).fetchone()
    return on_hand - reserved


def checkout(cart, idem_key, now, hold_seconds=600):
    """Reserve every line atomically and create a PENDING order whose prices are copied at this moment."""
    row = db.execute("SELECT id, status FROM orders WHERE idem_key = ?", (idem_key,)).fetchone()
    if row:
        return ("replay", row[0])                       # a retried request gets the same order back
    db.execute("BEGIN")
    try:
        total = 0
        for sku, qty in sorted(cart.items()):           # consistent order avoids deadlocks
            cur = db.execute("UPDATE product SET reserved = reserved + ? WHERE sku = ? AND on_hand - reserved >= ?", (qty, sku, qty))
            if cur.rowcount != 1:
                raise ValueError(f"not enough stock for {sku}")
        oid = db.execute("INSERT INTO orders (idem_key, status, expires_at) VALUES (?, 'PENDING', ?)", (idem_key, now + hold_seconds)).lastrowid
        for sku, qty in cart.items():
            price = db.execute("SELECT price_cents FROM product WHERE sku = ?", (sku,)).fetchone()[0]
            db.execute("INSERT INTO order_item VALUES (?, ?, ?, ?)", (oid, sku, qty, price))
            total += price * qty
        db.execute("UPDATE orders SET total_cents = ? WHERE id = ?", (total, oid))
        db.execute("COMMIT")
        return ("ok", oid)
    except ValueError as e:
        db.execute("ROLLBACK")
        return ("failed", str(e))


def _items(oid):
    return db.execute("SELECT sku, qty FROM order_item WHERE order_id = ?", (oid,)).fetchall()


def pay(oid, payment_ok):
    """Payment result arrives later. Success consumes the stock; failure compensates by releasing it."""
    status = db.execute("SELECT status FROM orders WHERE id = ?", (oid,)).fetchone()[0]
    if status != "PENDING":
        return status                                   # late or duplicate callback: ignore
    db.execute("BEGIN")
    for sku, qty in _items(oid):
        if payment_ok:
            db.execute("UPDATE product SET on_hand = on_hand - ?, reserved = reserved - ? WHERE sku = ?", (qty, qty, sku))
        else:
            db.execute("UPDATE product SET reserved = reserved - ? WHERE sku = ?", (qty, sku))
    new = "PAID" if payment_ok else "CANCELLED"
    db.execute("UPDATE orders SET status = ? WHERE id = ? AND status = 'PENDING'", (new, oid))
    db.execute("COMMIT")
    return new


def expire_holds(now):
    stale = [r[0] for r in db.execute("SELECT id FROM orders WHERE status = 'PENDING' AND expires_at <= ?", (now,)).fetchall()]
    for oid in stale:
        pay(oid, payment_ok=False)
    return len(stale)


if True:
    add_product("cable", 900, 5)
    add_product("lamp", 2500, 3)
    print("1.", checkout({"cable": 2, "lamp": 1}, "k1", now=0))
    print("2. retry of k1:", checkout({"cable": 2, "lamp": 1}, "k1", now=1))
    print("3. too many lamps:", checkout({"cable": 1, "lamp": 3}, "k2", now=2), "| cable available still", available("cable"), "(nothing half reserved)")
    print("4. pay order 1:", pay(1, True), "| duplicate callback:", pay(1, True), "| on_hand", db.execute("SELECT on_hand, reserved FROM product WHERE sku='lamp'").fetchone())
    ok = checkout({"cable": 3}, "k3", now=10)
    print("5. order", ok, "payment declined ->", pay(ok[1], False), "| cable available", available("cable"))
    ok = checkout({"cable": 3}, "k4", now=20, hold_seconds=600)
    print("6. abandoned checkout holds stock: available", available("cable"), "| expire at t=100:", expire_holds(100), "| at t=700:", expire_holds(700), "| available", available("cable"))
    db.execute("UPDATE product SET price_cents = 1200 WHERE sku = 'cable'")
    print("7. price changed to 1200, order 1 still shows:", db.execute("SELECT price_cents FROM order_item WHERE order_id = 1 AND sku = 'cable'").fetchone()[0])

    # flash sale: 100 units, 400 buyers. Naive = read stock, then write, with 10 buyers reading before any writes
    def flash(naive, seed=4):
        rnd = random.Random(seed)
        d = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
        d.isolation_level = None
        d.execute("CREATE TABLE s (stock INTEGER)")
        d.execute("INSERT INTO s VALUES (105)")
        sold = 0
        buyers = list(range(400))
        rnd.shuffle(buyers)
        for i in range(0, 400, 10):
            batch = buyers[i:i + 10]
            if naive:
                seen = [d.execute("SELECT stock FROM s").fetchone()[0] for _ in batch]       # all read first
                for st in seen:
                    if st > 0:
                        d.execute("UPDATE s SET stock = stock - 1")
                        sold += 1
            else:
                for _ in batch:
                    if d.execute("UPDATE s SET stock = stock - 1 WHERE stock > 0").rowcount == 1:
                        sold += 1
        return sold, d.execute("SELECT stock FROM s").fetchone()[0]
    print("flash sale of 105 units, 400 buyers, read-then-write:", flash(True), "(sold, stock left)")
    print("flash sale of 105 units, 400 buyers, conditional update:", flash(False))

## What the output shows

1. The first checkout reserves 2 cables and 1 lamp and creates order 1.
2. **Retrying the same idempotency key** returns **the same order** ("replay"): a client that timed out and retried does not buy twice.
3. A cart wanting **3 lamps** (only 2 left after the first order) **fails**, and cables show **3 available**, not 2: the reservation of the cable line was **rolled back** along with the failed line. Nothing is half reserved.
4. Paying order 1 moves it to **PAID** and consumes the stock (lamp: on hand 2, reserved 0). A **duplicate payment callback** is ignored (it still reports PAID, no double decrement).
5. A **declined payment** cancels order 2 and **releases** its stock: cable availability returns to 3. That is the **compensation**.
6. An **abandoned checkout** holds 3 cables (available drops to 0). The sweeper at time 100 finds nothing expired (the hold lasts 600 s); at time 700 it releases it, and **availability returns to 3**.
7. After the catalogue price changes to 1,200, order 1 **still shows 900**: the order copied the price when it was placed.
8. **Flash sale**: 105 units, 400 buyers, processed in groups of 10 who read the stock before any of them writes. With **read-then-write** the shop **sold 110 and the stock went to minus 5**: a group that all saw "5 left" all bought. With the **conditional update** (`WHERE stock > 0`) it **sold exactly 105 and ended at 0**.

The flash-sale race is a **replayed interleaving** (the browser runner cannot start threads), a faithful model of what concurrent requests do against a database without row-level protection. The sale size, the group size and the seed are fixed, so the numbers reproduce exactly.

## Flash sales

When thousands of buyers target one SKU, the problem shifts from correctness (the conditional update already gives that) to **throughput and fairness on one hot row**:

- **Admission control**: a **virtual waiting room** or token bucket lets a limited number of shoppers into checkout at a time; the rest see a queue position ([rate limiting](/sd/scalability/rate-limiting-algorithms/)).
- **Pre-allocate stock into tokens or buckets**: split the 100 units across N counters (or shards) so decrements hit different rows; a counter that is empty falls back to the others.
- **Queue the purchase attempts** and process them in order from a single consumer per SKU ([message queue case study](/sd/case-studies-data-and-transactions/design-a-distributed-message-queue/)); users get "you got it" or "sold out" asynchronously.
- **Serve the page from a CDN**, but never show a hard "in stock" claim from the cache: show "limited stock" and decide at reservation.
- **Fail fast**: once stock reaches zero, mark the SKU sold out in a cache so later requests stop before touching the database.

## Carts

Carts are **write-heavy and low-value**: a key-value store keyed by user or session id, with a TTL, is enough. Do **not** reserve stock when an item is added to a cart (carts are abandoned constantly); show availability as advisory and validate at checkout. On checkout, **re-read prices and stock** and tell the shopper about changes.

## Consistency choices

| data | consistency | why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inventory counts and reservations | strong (single-row atomic updates) | overselling is unacceptable |
| Orders and payments | strong, with idempotency | money and legal records |
| Catalogue and search | eventual | stale for seconds is fine |
| Cart | eventual, best effort | low cost of loss |
| "Only 3 left" badges | approximate | advisory only |

## Pitfalls

- **Read-then-write** stock checks (the demo oversold 5 units in one run).
- Reserving stock on **add to cart**, so abandoned carts starve real buyers.
- Reservations **without expiry**.
- No **idempotency key**: retries create duplicate orders or charges.
- **Compensations that can fail** with no retry, leaving stock reserved forever.
- Trusting a **payment callback** without checking the current order state (late or duplicate callbacks).
- Storing only a product id on the order, so the order's price **changes when the catalogue changes**.
- Showing **exact stock** from a cache and promising it.
- Doing the saga steps without **persisting state**, so a crash mid-checkout loses track of what to compensate.
- One **global lock** or table for all stock, making the whole shop contend.

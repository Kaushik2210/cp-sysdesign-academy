## The problem

Design a **distributed job scheduler**: users register **one-off jobs** ("run this at 14:00 tomorrow") and **recurring jobs** ("every night at 02:00"), and the system runs them **on time**, on a **fleet of workers**, **reliably** (a job is not silently lost), and **without running forever or twice by accident**. Think of cron, Kubernetes CronJobs, Airflow, or the scheduled-tasks feature of any SaaS product, but for **hundreds of millions of jobs**.

A single-machine cron is easy. The distributed problem is the interplay of three things: **time** (find the due jobs efficiently), **failure** (a scheduler or worker can die at any moment) and **duplicates** (the safe response to a failure is to retry, and retries run things twice).

## Requirements

**Functional**
- Create, update, pause and delete one-off and recurring (cron expression) jobs.
- Run each job at its scheduled time on a worker; record the outcome.
- **Retries** with backoff for failed runs; a **dead-letter** list for permanent failures.
- Priorities, per-tenant limits, and a way to see history and the next run time.

**Non-functional**
- **Timeliness**: start jobs within a second or two of their scheduled time (**lateness** is the key metric).
- **Reliability**: no job is lost; **at-least-once** execution.
- **Scalability**: 100 million jobs registered, thousands fired per second, a spike on every minute boundary.
- **No single point of failure**: schedulers and workers can crash and be replaced.

## Estimation

| quantity | assumption | result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Registered jobs | 100 million | about 100 million rows, 100 bytes each: 10 GB |
| Executions | 1 billion per day | about 11,600 per second on average |
| Minute-boundary spike | many jobs are set for "every hour on the hour" and the like; assume 10 times average | about 116,000 starts per second for a few seconds |
| Execution records | 1 billion per day x 200 bytes | about 200 GB per day (keep a short retention) |

The **job definitions** are small; the **execution history** is the large part and must expire. The central difficulty is the **spike**: jobs cluster on round times, so the scheduler must find and dispatch **tens of thousands of due jobs within a second**.

### Pieces

- **Job store**: the source of truth, sharded by job id. The key column is **`next_run_time`** with an index, so "all jobs due in the next 30 seconds in shard k" is a range scan.
- **Scheduler nodes**: each owns a **set of shards** by holding a **lease** from a coordination service ([leases and fencing](/sd/consensus-and-coordination/leases-locks-and-fencing/)). It periodically loads the jobs due in the **next window** into an **in-memory timer**, and when one fires it **enqueues a run**. If a scheduler dies, its leases expire and another node takes over its shards. The lease **fencing token** stops a slow, paused ex-owner from enqueueing duplicates.
- **Run queue**: durable, with **visibility timeouts** and priorities ([message queue case study](/sd/case-studies-data-and-transactions/design-a-distributed-message-queue/)). Decouples **deciding** that a job is due from **executing** it, absorbs the minute-boundary spike, and gives backpressure.
- **Workers** take a run with a **lease** (the visibility timeout), execute it, and **acknowledge**. They renew the lease for long jobs.
- **Recurring jobs**: when a run finishes (or when it is dispatched, depending on policy) the next `next_run_time` is computed from the cron expression and written back.
- **Retries**: a failed run is re-queued with **exponential backoff and jitter**; after a maximum number of attempts it goes to a **dead-letter queue** and raises an alert ([backpressure, retries and DLQ](/sd/queues-and-streaming/backpressure-retries-and-dlq/)).

## Finding due jobs fast: heap versus timing wheel

Inside a scheduler node the timers live in memory. The natural structure is a **priority queue** keyed by due time: **O(log n)** per insertion and per expiry. A **hashed timing wheel** is a circular array of slots, one per tick: a timer goes into slot (due time mod number of slots) with a count of full turns remaining; each tick looks **only at its own slot**. Insertion is **O(1)**, expiry is O(1) amortised, which suits huge numbers of short timers (it is what network stacks and Kafka's delay queues use). **Hierarchical** wheels (seconds, minutes, hours) cover long horizons without re-scanning.

## Executing safely: leases, failures and duplicates

A worker that **crashes after doing the work but before acknowledging** leaves the job's lease to expire, and the queue **gives the job to another worker**. This is the correct, safe behaviour (the alternative loses jobs), and it means **a job can run more than once**. This is **at-least-once** delivery. The effect of running twice (two emails, two charges) is prevented not by the scheduler but by **idempotent jobs**: each run carries a stable **run id** (job id plus scheduled time), and the side effect is recorded under that id so a repeat is recognised and skipped ([delivery semantics and idempotency](/sd/queues-and-streaming/delivery-semantics-and-idempotency/)).

## The simulation

The program has three parts:

1. **Heap versus timing wheel**: 50,000 timers due within 3,000 ticks, run through a heap (counting every comparison) and through two timing wheels (256 and 4,096 slots), checking that **all three fire exactly the same jobs at exactly the same ticks** and counting the work.
2. **Lease-based execution with crashing workers**: 2,000 jobs, at most 50 claimed per tick, a lease of 5 ticks. Workers **crash after doing the work but before acknowledging** with probability 0%, 10% or 30%. It runs each case **with and without an idempotent side effect**, counting executions, side effects and jobs affected twice or more.
3. **Catch-up policies** after a scheduler outage.

import random


class CountingKey:
    """Wraps a due time so that every comparison made by the heap is counted."""
    comparisons = 0
    __slots__ = ("t", "job")
    def __init__(self, t, job):
        self.t, self.job = t, job
    def __lt__(self, other):
        CountingKey.comparisons += 1
        return (self.t, self.job) < (other.t, other.job)


def heap_schedule(timers, horizon):
    """A priority queue of (due tick, job). Inserting and popping each cost O(log n) comparisons."""
    CountingKey.comparisons = 0
    h, fired = [], {}
    for job, t in timers:
        heapq.heappush(h, CountingKey(t, job))
    for tick in range(horizon + 1):
        while h and h[0].t <= tick:
            k = heapq.heappop(h)
            fired.setdefault(tick, []).append(k.job)
    return fired, CountingKey.comparisons


def wheel_schedule(timers, horizon, slots=256):
    """A hashed timing wheel: slot = due tick mod slots, each timer remembers how many full turns remain.
    Inserting is one append; each tick looks only at its own slot."""
    wheel = [[] for _ in range(slots)]
    ops, fired = 0, {}
    for job, t in timers:
        wheel[t % slots].append((t // slots, t, job))
        ops += 1
    for tick in range(horizon + 1):
        slot = wheel[tick % slots]
        ops += 1                                              # look at one slot
        keep = []
        for rounds, t, job in slot:
            ops += 1
            if t == tick:
                fired.setdefault(tick, []).append(job)
            else:
                keep.append((rounds, t, job))
        slot[:] = keep
    return fired, ops


def run_jobs(n_jobs, workers, crash_rate, lease, seed, idempotent):
    """Lease based execution: a worker claims a job and has 'lease' ticks to acknowledge it. A worker that crashes
    after doing the work but before acknowledging leaves the job to be claimed again when the lease expires."""
    rnd = random.Random(seed)
    state = {j: "ready" for j in range(n_jobs)}                  # ready | leased | done
    lease_until, executions, effects, done_tick = {}, 0, {}, {}
    sink_seen = set()
    tick = 0
    while any(s != "done" for s in state.values()):
        tick += 1
        for j, s in state.items():                                # expired leases make jobs claimable again
            if s == "leased" and lease_until[j] <= tick:
                state[j] = "ready"
        claimed = 0
        for j, s in state.items():
            if s == "ready" and claimed < workers:
                claimed += 1
                state[j] = "leased"
                lease_until[j] = tick + lease
                executions += 1
                if not idempotent or j not in sink_seen:          # the side effect, e.g. sending an email or charging a card
                    effects[j] = effects.get(j, 0) + 1
                    sink_seen.add(j)
                if rnd.random() < crash_rate:
                    continue                                      # crashed before acknowledging: the lease will expire
                state[j] = "done"
                done_tick[j] = tick
    return tick, executions, sum(effects.values()), sum(1 for v in effects.values() if v > 1)


def catch_up(missed, policy):
    """A job meant to run every minute; the scheduler was down and missed 'missed' runs."""
    return {"run every missed run": missed, "run once": 1, "skip to the next slot": 0}[policy]

## What the output shows

**Timers.** All three structures fired **exactly the same jobs at the same ticks** (`True`). The work differs:

| structure | work per timer |
| --- | --- |
| heap | **16.6** comparisons |
| timing wheel, 256 slots | **7.5** operations |
| timing wheel, 4,096 slots | **2.1** operations |

A wheel of 256 slots must revisit each timer once per turn of the wheel (due times spread over about 12 turns), which is why it costs more than the 4,096-slot wheel, whose single turn already covers the whole 3,000-tick horizon. The lesson: **a wheel pays off when its size is matched to the timer horizon**, and **hierarchical wheels** give that match for long horizons without a huge array. The heap is simpler and fine for far fewer timers; a counted-operations table is not a timing benchmark.

**Execution with crashes** (2,000 jobs):

| worker crash rate | idempotent side effect | executions | side effects | jobs affected 2 or more times | finished in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0% | no | 2,000 | 2,000 | 0 | 40 ticks |
| 10% | no | 2,200 | 2,200 | **183** | 52 ticks |
| 10% | **yes** | 2,200 | **2,000** | **0** | 52 ticks |
| 30% | no | 2,828 | 2,828 | **595** | 81 ticks |
| 30% | **yes** | 2,828 | **2,000** | **0** | 81 ticks |

- **No job was lost** at any crash rate: the lease mechanism guarantees every job eventually completes. That is the **at-least-once** guarantee.
- The **cost of safety is duplicates**: at a 10% crash rate, **183 jobs** (about 9%) had their side effect applied **twice or more**, and at 30% it was **595** (about 30%).
- Making the side effect **idempotent** (recording the run id) **removed every duplicate effect** (0 jobs affected twice) even though the same number of **executions** still happened: the work is repeated, but its **effect** is applied once. This is "exactly-once effect" built from at-least-once delivery plus idempotency.
- **Failures cost time**: finishing takes 40, 52 and 81 ticks as the crash rate rises, because each crash wastes a whole lease period.

The crash model is deliberately simple (independent crashes after the work). Real failures correlate and the lease length is a tuning trade-off: **too short** and healthy slow jobs are taken over and run twice; **too long** and a crashed job waits needlessly.

**Catch-up after an outage.** A once-a-minute job and a 90-minute scheduler outage leaves **90 missed runs**. The policy decides what happens next: **run every missed run** (90 runs: right for backfilling data pipelines, dangerous for "send the daily email"), **run once** (1 run: usually right for "refresh the cache"), or **skip to the next slot** (0 runs: right for time-sensitive jobs where a late run is useless). Make it a **per-job setting** with a **starting deadline** (do not run if later than X).

## Time, cron and daylight saving

- Store schedules and next run times in **UTC**, and keep the **time zone** of the job for evaluating cron expressions.
- **Daylight saving changes** create local times that **do not exist** (02:30 on the spring-forward night) or **happen twice** (the autumn night). Decide a policy and document it: usually skip or shift the missing run, and run the repeated time once.
- **Thundering herd at the minute boundary**: millions of jobs at :00. Add **jitter** where the user allows it, **spread dispatch over a few seconds** with the queue as a buffer, and **partition** schedulers so each handles a slice.
- **Clock skew** between schedulers can make a job fire a little early or late; the **job store's time** (a single authority) is used for deciding "due".

## Other design points

- **Exactly one scheduler per shard**: the lease prevents two schedulers firing the same job, but a **dual-ownership** window after a pause can happen; the **fencing token** plus **conditional updates** on the job row ("claim this run if it is still scheduled") make a double enqueue harmless.
- **Overlap policy** for recurring jobs: if the previous run is still going when the next is due, **skip**, **queue**, or **allow concurrent runs**.
- **Priorities and fairness**: separate queues per priority and per-tenant concurrency limits so one tenant's backfill cannot starve others.
- **Long-running jobs** need **heartbeats** that renew the lease; a job that stops heartbeating is considered dead.
- **Observability**: **lateness** (actual minus scheduled start), queue depth, run duration, failure rate, and the age of the oldest due-but-unstarted job ([observability](/sd/observability/logs-metrics-and-traces/)).

## Pitfalls

- **Running a job before the work is durable** or acknowledging before running, so crashes lose jobs.
- **Non-idempotent jobs behind an at-least-once scheduler**: duplicate emails and charges.
- **No fencing**: a paused old scheduler or worker wakes up and acts on stale ownership.
- **One scheduler for everything**: a single point of failure and a bottleneck.
- **Ignoring the minute-boundary spike**: workers and the database overloaded at :00.
- **Wrong catch-up behaviour** after outages (hundreds of backfilled runs at once).
- **Local time arithmetic** without time zones and daylight saving rules.
- **Leases that are too short or too long** for the job duration.
- **Unlimited retries** without backoff, a retry storm on a failing dependency ([retry amplification](/sd/microservice-patterns/retry-amplification-and-circuit-breakers/)).
- **Keeping all execution history forever**.
- Measuring only success, not **lateness**.

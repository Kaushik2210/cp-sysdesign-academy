## Intuition

The previous module ended on a hard question: how can several machines agree on *one* leader, *one* order of writes, or *one* value, when any of them may crash and any message may be late? Quorums and fencing tokens stop damage, but they do not by themselves choose a single answer. **Consensus** is the problem of making a group of nodes agree on a value in a way that survives failures, and a consensus **protocol** is the algorithm that does it.

Consensus is the foundation underneath many systems you rely on: the metadata store that holds a Kubernetes cluster's state (etcd), the lock service behind many large systems (Chubby, ZooKeeper), the replicated logs of distributed SQL databases, and leader election in practically every replicated service. If you need *one machine's behaviour* from *several machines*, consensus is how.

## The idea: a replicated state machine

Suppose every node starts in the same state and runs the same deterministic program. If they all apply the **same commands in the same order**, they all end in the same state, and any of them can answer reads or take over from a failed peer. That is a **replicated state machine**. The hard part is therefore reduced to one thing: agree on the **order of commands**, a shared **log**.

```text
client -> leader: "set x = 5"
leader appends to its log, copies the entry to followers
once a MAJORITY has stored it, the entry is COMMITTED
every node applies committed entries, in log order, to its state machine
```

## What a consensus protocol promises

- **Agreement (safety):** no two nodes decide different values.
- **Validity:** the decided value was proposed by some node (it is not invented).
- **Termination (liveness):** every working node eventually decides.

The FLP result says that in a fully asynchronous network, where message delay has no bound, no deterministic protocol can guarantee all three if even one node may crash. Practical protocols such as Raft and Paxos choose **safety always, liveness when the network behaves**: they never give a wrong answer, but during a prolonged partition or a very unlucky timing pattern they may stall for a while.

## Why majorities

Consensus protocols require a **majority (quorum)** of nodes to agree on each step. Two majorities of the same set always overlap in at least one node, so a decision made by one majority is visible to any later majority, and the group cannot "forget" it. With `n = 2f + 1` nodes a majority is `f + 1`, so the cluster survives **`f` crashed nodes**:

| nodes | majority | failures tolerated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | 2 | 1 |
| 5 | 3 | 2 |
| 7 | 4 | 3 |

Even sizes add cost without adding tolerance: 4 nodes need a majority of 3 and still tolerate only 1 failure, so clusters use odd sizes. More nodes mean more failures tolerated but slower writes (more acknowledgements), which is why 3 or 5 is typical.

## Raft in one page

Raft was designed to be understandable. It splits consensus into three sub-problems: **leader election**, **log replication** and **safety**.

### Roles and terms

Every node is a **follower**, a **candidate** or a **leader**. Time is divided into **terms**, numbered with increasing integers. Each term begins with an election; there is at most one leader per term. A term number acts as a logical clock: a node that sees a higher term immediately steps down and updates its own.

### Leader election

Followers expect a periodic **heartbeat** (an empty append message) from the leader. If a follower hears nothing for its **election timeout**, it assumes the leader failed: it increments the term, becomes a candidate, votes for itself and asks the others for votes (`RequestVote`). Each node grants **one vote per term**, first come first served. A candidate that collects votes from a majority becomes the leader and immediately sends heartbeats to assert itself.

If several followers time out at nearly the same moment, they split the vote and nobody reaches a majority. The term simply ends with no leader and a new election starts. To make that rare, every node picks its election timeout **at random** from a range (the Raft paper suggests 150 to 300 ms), so one node usually times out first and wins before the others even notice.

### Log replication

The leader appends each client command to its log and sends `AppendEntries` to followers. Each message carries the index and term of the entry just before the new ones, and a follower accepts it only if its own log matches at that position. If not, the follower refuses, and the leader backs up and resends until the logs agree; from then on the follower's log is a copy of the leader's. An entry is **committed** once the leader has stored it on a majority; the leader then tells the followers, and everyone applies it to their state machine.

### Safety: why a committed entry is never lost

Raft adds two rules that make this work:

- **Election restriction:** a node votes for a candidate only if the candidate's log is **at least as up to date** as its own (compare the last entry's term, then its index). Since a committed entry is on a majority, and any winning candidate collected a majority of votes, the winner must contain every committed entry.
- **Commit rule:** a leader only counts entries from its **own term** as committed by counting replicas; earlier-term entries become committed indirectly once a later entry of the current term is committed.

Together these guarantee **leader completeness**: any leader has all committed entries, so committed entries are never overwritten.

## Reads, membership and performance

- **Reads:** reading from the leader's state machine without checking could return stale data if the node has been deposed without knowing it. Raft systems either run reads through the log, confirm leadership with a heartbeat round (a "read index"), or use time-based **leader leases** (which rely on bounded clock drift). Reads served by followers are cheaper but may be stale.
- **Membership changes:** adding or removing nodes must never create two disjoint majorities. Raft uses **joint consensus** (or one-at-a-time changes), so old and new configurations overlap during the switch.
- **Throughput:** the leader is the bottleneck. Real systems batch many commands per message, pipeline replication, and write the log to disk with group commit. Each commit costs about one network round trip plus a durable disk write on a majority.
- **Log growth:** nodes periodically take a **snapshot** of the state machine and discard the log up to that point; a very slow follower is brought up to date by sending it the snapshot.

## Leader election, simulated

The simulation below runs repeated Raft-style elections for 5 nodes after a leader failure, with one-way latency between 5 and 15 ms and a base election timeout of 150 ms plus a random **spread**. A split vote ends the round and everyone retries with new random timeouts. Elections that never produce a leader within 20 rounds are reported as failures.

<CodeRunner language="python" code={`import random

MAX_ROUNDS = 20

def election(n, base, spread, rng, lat=(5, 15)):
    """Repeat election rounds until some node gets a majority. Returns (rounds, total_time_ms)."""
    need = n // 2 + 1
    total = 0.0
    rounds = 0
    while rounds < MAX_ROUNDS:
        rounds += 1
        timeouts = sorted((base + rng.random() * spread, i) for i in range(n))
        voted = {}                     # node -> time it cast its vote in this round (self-vote or for a candidate)
        votes = {}                     # candidate -> list of times the votes reached it
        events = []                    # (time, kind, node, from)
        for t, i in timeouts:
            events.append((t, 0, i, i))                      # kind 0: timeout fires
        events.sort()
        # process events in time order; requests and replies are scheduled dynamically
        import heapq
        heap = list(events); heapq.heapify(heap)
        winner = None
        while heap and winner is None:
            t, kind, node, src = heapq.heappop(heap)
            if kind == 0:                                    # timeout: become candidate if no vote cast yet
                if node in voted: continue
                voted[node] = t                              # votes for itself
                votes.setdefault(node, []).append(t)
                for j in range(n):
                    if j != node:
                        heapq.heappush(heap, (t + rng.uniform(*lat), 1, j, node))   # RequestVote arrives
            elif kind == 1:                                  # request arrives at node from candidate src
                if node not in voted:
                    voted[node] = t
                    heapq.heappush(heap, (t + rng.uniform(*lat), 2, src, node))    # vote reply travels back
            else:                                            # a vote reaches candidate 'node'
                votes[node].append(t)
                if len(votes[node]) >= need:
                    winner = (node, t)
        if winner is not None:
            return rounds, total + winner[1]
        # split vote: the round times out and everyone tries again after a fresh random timeout
        total += base + spread + 2 * lat[1]
    return None, None                                        # no leader after MAX_ROUNDS rounds: livelock

def study(n, base, spread, trials=3000, seed=5):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    rs, ts, split, failed = [], [], 0, 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        r, t = election(n, base, spread, rng)
        if r is None:
            failed += 1
            continue
        rs.append(r); ts.append(t)
        if r > 1: split += 1
    ok = len(ts)
    if ok == 0:
        return None
    ts.sort()
    return sum(rs) / ok, sum(ts) / ok, ts[int(0.99 * ok)], 100 * split / ok, 100 * failed / trials

print("5 nodes, base timeout 150 ms, one-way latency 5-15 ms, 3000 elections after a leader failure (give up after 20 rounds)")
print("  timeout spread   avg rounds   avg time to leader   99th pct time   split-vote elections   never elected a leader")
for spread in (0, 5, 20, 50, 150):
    res = study(5, 150, spread)
    if res is None:
        print(f"  {spread:>10} ms   {'-':>10}   {'-':>18}   {'-':>13}   {'-':>20}   {100.0:>20.1f}%")
    else:
        r, t, p99, sp, fl = res
        print(f"  {spread:>10} ms   {r:>10.3f}   {t:>15.0f} ms   {p99:>10.0f} ms   {sp:>19.1f}%   {fl:>20.1f}%")

A local run with 3,000 elections per row printed: with a spread of **0 ms** or **5 ms** no election ever produced a leader within 20 rounds (100% livelock, because all nodes time out before any request arrives and each votes for itself); with **20 ms**, 55.4% of elections split the vote and the average time to a leader was 421 ms; with **50 ms**, 11.8% split and 208 ms; and with **150 ms**, the range the Raft paper recommends, only 1.5% split and the average was 200 ms with a 99th percentile of 517 ms. Randomising the timeout over a range comparable to or larger than the network delay is what makes elections finish.

## Failure modes

- **Leader crash:** a short unavailability while a new leader is elected (hundreds of milliseconds to seconds).
- **Minority partition:** the minority side cannot elect a leader or commit, so it stalls instead of diverging.
- **Disk stalls:** a slow disk on the leader looks like a failed leader and triggers elections; consensus is sensitive to disk latency.
- **Majority loss:** with 2 of 3 nodes down, the cluster stops; restoring needs manual recovery or bringing nodes back.
- **Timeout misconfiguration:** too tight, and normal pauses cause election storms; too loose, and failover is slow.
- **Large clusters:** each extra node slows commits and adds failure surface; consensus clusters stay small, and large datasets are sharded across many small groups.

## Pitfalls

- Running an even number of nodes and expecting extra tolerance.
- Putting all nodes in one availability zone, so one outage removes the majority.
- Storing large or frequently changing data in the consensus group; keep it for small, critical metadata.
- Serving reads from a follower or an old leader and assuming they are fresh.
- Forgetting that a client may not know whether a timed-out write was committed; commands need request ids.
- Treating consensus as a way to get availability; it trades availability on the minority side for safety.

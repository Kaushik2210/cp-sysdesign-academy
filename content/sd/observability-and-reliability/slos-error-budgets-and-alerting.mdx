## Intuition

"The site should be reliable" cannot be engineered. "99.9% of checkout requests succeed within 500 ms, measured monthly" can. Reliability work needs numbers that both engineers and the business agree on, a way to spend risk deliberately, and alerts that fire when users are actually being hurt. SLIs, SLOs and error budgets provide all three.

## Definitions

- **SLI (service level indicator)**: a measured ratio of good events to valid events. `successful requests / total requests`, or `requests faster than 500 ms / total requests`. Choose SLIs from the user's point of view (availability, latency, correctness, freshness, durability).
- **SLO (service level objective)**: the target for an SLI over a window: "99.9% of requests are good over 30 days".
- **SLA (service level agreement)**: a contract with consequences (credits, penalties). It is looser than the internal SLO (SLA 99.9%, SLO 99.95%), so you notice and fix problems before you breach the contract.
- **Error budget**: `1 - SLO`. A 99.9% SLO over 30 days allows 0.1% bad events, or about 43.2 minutes of full downtime.

## Availability numbers to know

| SLO | Allowed downtime per 30 days | Per year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 99% | 7.2 hours | 3.65 days |
| 99.9% | 43.2 minutes | 8.76 hours |
| 99.95% | 21.6 minutes | 4.38 hours |
| 99.99% | 4.3 minutes | 52.6 minutes |
| 99.999% | 26 seconds | 5.3 minutes |

Each extra nine costs roughly 10 times more: redundancy across zones and regions, automated failover, careful deploys, and on-call maturity. Your SLO should be **no higher than users need**, and cannot exceed the SLOs of your critical dependencies (a service that depends serially on three 99.9% services cannot honestly promise 99.99%).

```python
def budget_minutes(slo, days=30):
    return (1 - slo) * days * 24 * 60

for slo in (0.99, 0.999, 0.9995, 0.9999, 0.99999):
    print(slo, round(budget_minutes(slo), 1), "minutes per 30 days")
# 0.99 432.0, 0.999 43.2, 0.9995 21.6, 0.9999 4.3, 0.99999 0.4
```

## Using the error budget

The budget turns the reliability-versus-velocity argument into data:

- **Budget remaining:** ship features, run experiments, do risky migrations.
- **Budget exhausted:** freeze risky launches, spend time on reliability work until the window recovers.

This gives product and engineering a shared rule and makes "100% reliable" visibly the wrong goal: it would mean never changing anything.

## Choosing good SLIs

- Measure at the edge (load balancer, client) where users feel it, not only inside a pod.
- Define "good" precisely: HTTP status below 500 (4xx are the caller's fault), latency below a threshold, response correct.
- Use ratios of counts, not averages. Prefer a **latency SLI as the share of requests faster than X**, not "average latency".
- Keep to a few SLIs per user journey (checkout availability, checkout latency), not dozens.
- For batch and pipelines: freshness (data is no more than 10 minutes old) and correctness.

## Alerting on burn rate

**Burn rate** is how fast you consume the budget relative to the allowed pace. Burn rate 1 means you will use exactly the whole budget by the end of the window. Burn rate 14.4 means the whole 30-day budget would be gone in about 2 days.

If the error ratio over a window is `e` and the SLO error budget is `b = 1 - SLO`, then `burn_rate = e / b`.

Multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerts (from the SRE Workbook) page on fast burns and ticket on slow ones, and require **both a long and a short window** to be over the threshold so the alert resets quickly after recovery:

| Severity | Burn rate | Long window | Short window | Budget consumed if sustained |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Page | 14.4 | 1 hour | 5 minutes | 2% in an hour |
| Page | 6 | 6 hours | 30 minutes | 5% in six hours |
| Ticket | 1 | 3 days | 6 hours | 10% over three days |

```python
def burn_rate(error_ratio, slo):
    return error_ratio / (1 - slo)

def should_page(err_1h, err_5m, slo=0.999, threshold=14.4):
    return burn_rate(err_1h, slo) >= threshold and burn_rate(err_5m, slo) >= threshold

print(round(burn_rate(0.0144, 0.999), 1))     # 14.4: a 1.44% error rate on a 99.9% SLO
print(should_page(0.0150, 0.0200))            # True: sustained and still happening
print(should_page(0.0150, 0.0005))            # False: it was bad an hour ago but has recovered
# days to exhaust a 30-day budget at burn rate r: 30 / r
print(30 / 14.4, 30 / 6, 30 / 1)              # about 2.1 days, 5 days, 30 days
```

## Good alerting principles

- **Page only for symptoms that need a human now** and are about user impact (SLO burn), not causes (CPU, a single pod restart). Every page should be urgent, actionable and novel.
- Non-urgent signals become tickets or dashboard annotations, not pages.
- Attach a **runbook link** and context (dashboards, recent deploys) to every alert.
- Track alert quality: pages per on-call shift, share that were actionable. More than a couple of pages per 12-hour shift causes burnout and missed real incidents.
- Keep a **cause-based** alert only when it gives lead time (disk will be full in 4 hours).

## Worked example

Checkout has an SLO of 99.9% good requests over 30 days (budget 43.2 minutes of equivalent downtime). A bad deploy raises the error rate to 5% for 20 minutes. At 5% the burn rate is 50. The fast-burn alert pages within minutes; the on-call rolls back. The incident consumed `20 min × 5% = 1` minute of full-outage equivalent, about 2.3% of the monthly budget, so there is still plenty to ship new features. Compare a slow leak of 0.3% errors for a week: burn rate 3, no page, but the ticket alert flags it before it quietly eats the whole budget.

## Pitfalls

- SLOs set by wishful thinking rather than user need and measured capability.
- Measuring only in the service itself (it can be "up" while the load balancer returns errors).
- Threshold alerts on raw error counts that fire at low traffic or miss high traffic.
- No consequence when the budget is exhausted, so the SLO is decoration.
- Setting the SLA equal to the SLO, with no safety margin.
- Counting planned maintenance inconsistently.

## What would you choose and why?

A search API has an availability SLO of 99.95% and a latency SLO (p99 under 300 ms). Its main dependency is a database cluster with a 99.9% SLA. Is the 99.95% achievable? What design changes (caching, replicas, fallbacks) could make it honest, and what would you tell the product team?

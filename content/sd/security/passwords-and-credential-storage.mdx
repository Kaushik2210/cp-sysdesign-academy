## Intuition

Sooner or later a database leaks: through a bug, a stolen backup, an insider, a misconfigured bucket. The question that decides how bad the day is: **what did you store in the password column?**

- **Plaintext:** every account is compromised at once, and so are the same users' accounts on other sites, because people reuse passwords.
- **A fast hash (plain SHA-256 or MD5):** a modern GPU computes billions of such hashes per second, so an attacker simply tries a dictionary of common passwords and then every short combination. Most real passwords fall within hours.
- **A slow, salted, purpose-built hash (Argon2id, scrypt, bcrypt, PBKDF2):** each guess costs the attacker real time and memory, and each user must be attacked separately. Weak passwords still fall, but strong ones do not.

The goal of credential storage is not to make a leak harmless, it is to make the leaked data **expensive to turn back into passwords**.

## The three ingredients

1. **Slowness (a work factor).** A password hash is deliberately expensive: PBKDF2 repeats an HMAC many thousand times, bcrypt uses an expensive key setup, and scrypt and **Argon2id** also demand large amounts of **memory**, which blunts GPUs and custom hardware. Tune the cost so that one verification takes on the order of 100 to 500 ms on your servers, and raise it over time. (As an example, the OWASP cheat sheet at the time of writing suggests Argon2id with 19 MiB of memory, 2 iterations and 1 lane as a minimum, or PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 with 600,000 iterations; check the current guidance.)
2. **A unique random salt per password**, stored next to the hash. It defeats precomputed tables (rainbow tables), prevents an attacker from cracking all users who share a password with one pass, and hides *who has the same password*.
3. **A strong algorithm used through a library.** Never design your own scheme such as `sha256(password + secret)` or double hashing.

Optional extras: a **pepper**, a secret value kept outside the database (in an HSM or secret manager) and mixed into the hash, so a database-only leak is not enough; and **upgrading hashes on login** (re-hash with new parameters when the user next signs in).

## Beyond storage

- **Rate limit and monitor logins** per account and per IP; use exponential back-off or CAPTCHAs; alert on credential stuffing patterns (many accounts, one password).
- **Multi-factor authentication (MFA):** TOTP codes, push approvals, hardware keys. Prefer **phishing-resistant** factors (WebAuthn security keys and **passkeys**), which bind the credential to the real website.
- **Check new passwords against breach lists** and enforce a sensible minimum length (NIST recommends length over arbitrary composition rules, and no forced periodic changes without evidence of compromise).
- **Do not reveal whether an account exists:** use the same message and timing for "unknown user" and "wrong password"; do a dummy hash for unknown users.
- **Constant-time comparison** of secrets, to avoid timing leaks.
- **Secure reset flows:** single-use, short-lived, random tokens sent to a verified channel; invalidate sessions after a reset.
- **Delegate** where you can: sign-in with an identity provider (OpenID Connect) or passkeys removes password storage from your threat model entirely.

## Code: why slow and salted matters

The program has two parts. The first computes how long it takes to try *every* password of a given kind at two guess rates: a fast unsalted hash on a GPU (`10^10` guesses per second) and PBKDF2 with 100,000 iterations (`10^5` guesses per second for the same hardware, because each guess now costs 100,000 hashes). The second builds a database of 500 users, 60% of whom picked a password from a 300-word dictionary, and attacks it with that dictionary, once against unsalted hashes and once against salted ones, counting real SHA-256 operations.

### Measured results

Running this exact program with Python 3.13 produced:

| password kind | search space | fast hash (`10^10`/s) | PBKDF2 100k iterations (`10^5`/s) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 8 lowercase letters | `26^8` | 0.0058 hours | 580 hours |
| 8 letters and digits (mixed case) | `62^8` | 6.07 hours | 69.2 years |
| 8 of all 94 printable characters | `94^8` | 169 hours | 1,930 years |
| 4 random dictionary words | `7776^4` | 102 hours | 1,160 years |
| 6 random dictionary words | `7776^6` | 701,000 years | 70 billion years |

| 500 users, 300-word dictionary | users cracked | hash operations | users sharing a hash with another |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| unsalted | 312 | 300 | 243 |
| salted (unique 64-bit salts) | 312 | 71,851 | 0 |

Two lessons. **First, the work factor changes the answer by orders of magnitude**: an 8-character mixed-case password falls in about 6 hours to a fast hash and takes about 69 years against 100,000-iteration PBKDF2 (the same brute-force search, one hundred thousand times slower per guess: 10^10 against 10^5 guesses per second). Length and randomness matter just as much: six random words are out of reach under either scheme. **Second, salting does not protect the users who chose guessable passwords** (the same 312 users fell in both cases), because a salt does not make a weak password strong. What it does is raise the attacker's cost: 240 times more hash operations for just 500 users (and the gap grows linearly with the number of users), and it hides the fact that 243 users shared a password with someone else, a useful clue for targeted attacks. The attack rates are round numbers chosen for illustration and real hardware varies; use them to compare *kinds* of storage and passwords, not as predictions.

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not roll your own">
A hand-made scheme such as `sha256(salt + password)` is salted but still fast. Use a maintained password-hashing library with its recommended parameters, and store the parameters with each hash so you can raise them later.
</Callout>

## Pitfalls

- Storing plaintext, reversible encryption or unsalted fast hashes.
- A single global salt (it behaves like a pepper stored in the database, and does not stop per-user tables).
- Choosing a work factor once and never revisiting it as hardware improves.
- Different error messages or response times for "no such user" and "wrong password".
- Letting an attacker try unlimited passwords, or locking accounts after a few failures so that anyone can lock out anyone.
- Logging passwords, or including them in URLs or error reports.
- Using the password hash function for API keys or tokens: long random secrets can use a fast hash, passwords cannot.
- Security questions as password recovery ("mother's maiden name" is public information).

## Intuition

Capacity planning answers: *how many of each resource do we need to meet demand with acceptable latency, even when part of the system fails?* It is back-of-envelope estimation plus three correction factors people forget: peak versus average, headroom, and failure.

## The method

1. **Estimate demand**: daily users, actions per user, therefore average requests per second (`daily_requests / 86,400`).
2. **Apply a peak factor**: traffic is bursty. A common rule of thumb is 2x to 5x average; measure your own.
3. **Measure per-unit capacity**: requests per second one instance sustains within the latency target, found by load testing, not by guessing.
4. **Apply a utilisation target**: do not plan to run at 100%. Queueing delay rises sharply as utilisation approaches 1, so 50% to 70% of tested max is typical.
5. **Add failure headroom**: survive the loss of a zone or a node (N+1, or N+1 zone).
6. **Do it for every tier**: app, cache, database reads and writes, network, storage, and third-party quotas.
7. **Add growth**: project 6 to 12 months and plan lead time for procurement or quota increases.

<Callout type="note">
Why not run at 90%? In a simple queue, mean waiting time scales like `utilisation / (1 - utilisation)`. Going from 50% to 90% utilisation multiplies queueing delay by 9. Tail latency is hit even harder.
</Callout>

## Worked example: a social feed API

Assumptions: 20 million daily active users; each opens the app 5 times a day, and each open makes 2 feed API calls: 10 calls per user per day. 90% of calls are reads, 10% writes (likes, posts).

**Demand**

- Daily calls: 20M x 10 = 200M. Average: 200M / 86,400 = about 2,300 requests per second.
- Peak factor 3x: about 7,000 requests per second. Reads 6,300, writes 700.

**App tier**

- Load test shows one instance sustains 250 requests per second at p99 under 200 ms.
- Utilisation target 60%: plan 150 requests per second per instance.
- 7,000 / 150 = about 47 instances.
- Survive loss of one of three zones: the remaining two zones must carry the peak, so total capacity must be 47 x 3/2, about 70. Round to 72 instances, 24 per zone.

**Cache and database**

- Cache hit rate target 90% on reads: 6,300 x 10% = 630 reads per second reach the database, plus 700 writes: about 1,330 operations per second.
- If a single database primary handles about 5,000 simple operations per second in your tests, this fits with headroom of about 3.8x. Add read replicas for availability, and plan cache capacity: if the hot working set is 50 GB with replication factor 2, you need at least 100 GB of cache memory plus overhead.

**Storage**

- Each write stores 1 KB of metadata. 20M x 10 x 10% = 20M writes per day, about 20 GB per day, 7.3 TB per year before replication and indexes. With 3 replicas and about 30% index overhead, plan roughly 28 TB for a year.

**Third parties**

- A push-notification provider caps at 2,000 requests per second. If peak events could produce 5,000, you need batching or a queue with a drain rate matching the quota.

## Pitfalls

- **Planning from averages** and being surprised by peaks, retries and cache-cold events.
- **Using vendor "max" numbers** instead of your own load tests with realistic payloads.
- **Sizing the app tier but not the database, cache, or quota**; the narrowest tier sets your limit.
- **Forgetting retries**: during an incident, retries may double traffic.
- **Ignoring failure headroom**: capacity that exactly matches peak fails on the first node loss.
- **Not revisiting**: workloads change; review quarterly and after launches.

## What would you choose and why?

Using the numbers above, the product team announces a campaign expected to double daily active users in a month, concentrated in a single country that sits in one region. Which tier hits its limit first? What would you load test, what would you pre-scale, and what would you put behind rate limits or shedding rather than buying capacity for?

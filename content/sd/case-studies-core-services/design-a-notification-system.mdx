## The problem

Design a **notification system** that other services call to tell users things: a **one-time passcode** by SMS, a **"your order shipped"** push, a **weekly digest** email, a **"someone liked your photo"** in-app alert. It supports several **channels** (mobile push, SMS, email, in-app) and must respect what each user has **opted into**.

The surprise in this problem is that the happy path is trivial (call a provider's API) and almost everything else is about **failure and restraint**: not losing the passcode, not sending 500 notifications for 500 likes, not letting a marketing blast delay a login code, surviving a provider outage, and never contacting someone who opted out.

## 1. Requirements

**Functional**

- Other services submit a **notification request** (user or segment, type, parameters) through an API or an event.
- Deliver through **push, SMS, email and in-app**, choosing channels by **user preference** and notification type.
- **Templates** with localisation and variables; **scheduled** and **recurring** sends; **bulk campaigns** to millions of users.
- Honour **opt-outs**, **quiet hours** and per-type preferences; give delivery **status** and basic analytics.

**Non-functional**

- **Reliable**: a passcode or a security alert must not be lost (at-least-once delivery), and nothing should be sent twice to the user.
- **Latency by class**: passcodes in seconds; transactional updates in seconds to a minute; marketing in minutes to hours.
- **Scales** with large spikes (a campaign to 50 million users).
- **Protects users and providers**: rate limits, no spamming, compliance with consent rules.

## 2. Estimation

Assume 100 million users receiving an average of 5 notifications a day, plus a campaign to 50 million users in one hour.

<CodeRunner language="python" code={`users, per_user_per_day = 100_000_000, 5
per_day = users * per_user_per_day

The average (about 5,800 a second) is easy. The **campaign** is what hurts: about 14,000 a second, and at the provider's pace it can take **longer than the hour you wanted**. Anything else sharing the same pipeline queues up **behind** it, so the design must **isolate traffic classes**.

**The flow of one request**

1. **Accept and de-duplicate.** The caller sends a request with an **idempotency key**; the API records it (a key-value store with a TTL) so a retried call does not send twice.
2. **Decide whether and how.** The **preference service** answers: is this user opted in to this type, on which channels, is it quiet hours in their time zone, are they on the suppression list (unsubscribed, bounced, complained)?
3. **Render.** The **template service** fills in language-specific text with escaped variables.
4. **Limit and collapse.** Per-user limits and collapsing rules decide whether this becomes a message, is merged into a digest, or is dropped.
5. **Enqueue by priority.** The message goes to the queue for its class; a durable queue gives at-least-once delivery.
6. **Send.** Channel workers call the provider, handle the response and **retry** transient failures with backoff.
7. **Track.** Providers report delivery, bounces and opens through callbacks; the status store updates.

## 4. Isolating priorities

If everything shares one first-in-first-out queue, a big marketing blast sits in front of a login code. The simulation sends 400,000 marketing messages in 10 seconds plus 3,000 one-time passcodes spread over a minute, to senders that can push 1,000 messages a second, with one shared queue versus separate priority queues.

<CodeRunner language="python" timeoutMs={30000} code={`import heapq, random

def run(prioritised, seed=3):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    capacity = 1000                                   # messages the senders can push per second
    events = []                                       # (arrival second, kind)
    for _ in range(400000):                           # a marketing blast: 400,000 messages arrive within 10 seconds
        events.append((rng.random() * 10, "marketing"))
    for _ in range(3000):                             # one-time passcodes arrive steadily over 60 seconds
        events.append((rng.random() * 60, "otp"))
    events.sort()
    waits, i, t, heap = [], 0, 0.0, []
    while i < len(events) or heap:
        if not heap and i < len(events) and events[i][0] > t:
            t = events[i][0]
        while i < len(events) and events[i][0] <= t:
            arrival, kind = events[i]
            priority = (0 if kind == "otp" else 1) if prioritised else 0
            heapq.heappush(heap, (priority, arrival, kind))
            i += 1
        if heap:
            priority, arrival, kind = heapq.heappop(heap)
            t += 1 / capacity
            if kind == "otp":
                waits.append(t - arrival)
    return sorted(waits)

for prioritised in (False, True):
    w = run(prioritised)
    label = "separate priority for OTP" if prioritised else "one shared FIFO queue     "
    print(f"{label}: passcode wait  median {w[len(w) // 2] * 1000:9,.0f} ms   p99 {w[int(len(w) * 0.99)] * 1000:9,.0f} ms   max {w[-1] * 1000:9,.0f} ms")
`} />

With one shared queue the median passcode waited **about six minutes** (the p99 was about 390 seconds), long after the user gave up and requested another. With a separate high-priority queue the same passcodes waited about **2 milliseconds**, little more than the time to send one message. In production use **separate queues (or topics) and separate worker pools per class**, not just a priority flag in one queue, so a flood of low-priority work cannot even occupy the workers; and reserve capacity so critical traffic never needs to wait. (Strict priority can starve the low class if the high class is constantly busy; guard with weighted shares or a minimum rate.)

## 5. Retries, failover and idempotency

Providers fail: APNs and FCM throttle, an SMS carrier goes down, an email provider returns `5xx`. Rules:

- **Retry transient failures** (timeouts, `5xx`, throttling) with **exponential backoff and jitter**, up to a limit, then send to a **dead-letter queue** and alert. Do not retry permanent failures (invalid token, unknown number, hard email bounce): remove the token or add the address to the **suppression list** instead.
- Put a **circuit breaker** on each provider ([retry amplification and circuit breakers](/sd/microservice-patterns/retry-amplification-and-circuit-breakers/)) so a down provider does not tie up all your workers.
- Keep **more than one provider per critical channel** (SMS, email) and fail over automatically.
- Make sends **idempotent** with an id the provider accepts where possible, so a retry after a timeout (the first call may have succeeded) does not double-send.

The simulation sends 10,000 messages over 100 seconds while the primary provider is **down from second 20 to second 80** and fails 2% of calls at other times.

<CodeRunner language="python" timeoutMs={30000} code={`import random

def send_all(n, use_backup, retries, seed=11):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    def provider_ok(name, t):
        if name == "primary":
            return not (20 <= t < 80) and rng.random() > 0.02       # outage from t=20 to t=80, 2% random failures
        return rng.random() > 0.01
    delivered, latencies = 0, []
    for k in range(n):
        t0 = k * 100 / n                                             # messages arrive evenly over 100 seconds
        t, ok = t0, False
        for attempt in range(retries + 1):
            if provider_ok("primary", t):
                ok = True
                break
            if use_backup and provider_ok("backup", t):
                ok = True
                break
            t += min(30, 2 ** attempt) * (0.5 + rng.random())         # exponential backoff with jitter
        if ok:
            delivered += 1
            latencies.append(t - t0)
    latencies.sort()
    return delivered / n, latencies[len(latencies) // 2], latencies[int(len(latencies) * 0.99)]

for use_backup, retries, label in ((False, 0, "primary only, no retry"), (False, 5, "primary only, 5 retries with backoff"), (True, 5, "primary + backup provider, 5 retries")):
    rate, p50, p99 = send_all(10000, use_backup, retries)
    print(f"{label:40s} delivered {rate:6.1%}   latency median {p50:5.1f} s   p99 {p99:6.1f} s")
`} />

During a 60-second outage in a 100-second window the results were:

- **No retry**: only **39.2%** delivered (the outage swallowed the rest).
- **Retries with backoff** raised that to **70.4%**, with a p99 delay of about **40 seconds** for the messages that had to wait. Retries cannot save messages whose whole retry budget falls inside the outage.
- **A second provider** delivered **100.0%** with essentially no added delay.

A backup provider fixes provider outages but adds cost, a second integration and the need to keep sender identities, templates and deliverability reputation warm on both.

## 6. Not annoying users: preferences, limits and collapsing

- **Preferences:** per type and channel, with global **quiet hours** in the user's own time zone, and always honour **unsubscribe** immediately (legal requirements apply in many places; the suppression list is checked on every send).
- **Per-user rate limits** ([algorithms](/sd/scalability/rate-limiting-algorithms/)): at most N notifications per hour, with higher caps for critical types.
- **Collapsing and digests:** merge many similar events into one. A "collapse key" per `(user, type, object)` lets the system replace a pending notification instead of adding another.

<CodeRunner language="python" code={`import random

rng = random.Random(7)
likes = sorted(rng.random() * 600 for _ in range(500))        # 500 "someone liked your photo" events in 10 minutes

def collapse(times, window):
    sent, last = 0, None
    for t in times:
        if last is None or t - last >= window:
            sent += 1
            last = t
    return sent

for window in (0, 30, 120, 600):
    sent = collapse(likes, window) if window else len(likes)
    print(f"at most one notification per {window:3d} s: {sent:3d} notifications for {len(likes)} events")
`} />

A 30-second window turns **500 events into 20 notifications**, and 2 minutes into 5. The trade-off is **delay**: a collapsing notification is sent at the start of its window and later events are folded into a count ("12 people liked your photo") or into the next digest.

## 7. Channel details that matter

- **Push (APNs, FCM):** keep a **device token registry** per user and device; tokens change and expire, so handle "unregistered" responses by deleting them. Use the provider's **priority** and **time-to-live** so a stale "your ride arrived" push is dropped. Payloads are small; for sensitive content send a signal and let the app fetch.
- **SMS:** the most expensive and most regulated channel (sender registration, opt-in proof, per-country rules, delivery varies by carrier). Use it for **passcodes and critical alerts**, not marketing by default. Protect it from **SMS pumping fraud** (attackers triggering expensive messages to premium numbers) with rate limits and country restrictions.
- **Email:** deliverability is its own discipline: **SPF, DKIM and DMARC** authentication, a good sender reputation, low bounce and spam-complaint rates, a one-click unsubscribe header, separate sending domains and IPs for transactional and marketing mail. Handle **hard bounces** (stop sending) and **complaints** (suppress) automatically.
- **In-app:** stored per user and delivered over the user's live connection or on next open ([chat system](/sd/case-studies-core-services/design-a-chat-system/) shows that machinery); keep a read/unread state.

## 8. Scheduling and campaigns

- **Delayed and recurring sends** use a **scheduler**: a persistent delayed queue or a time-bucketed store scanned by workers (a timer wheel in memory, backed by a database). Make triggering idempotent, because two schedulers may fire the same job.
- **Campaigns to segments** are **expanded gradually** (page through the audience and enqueue in batches) so the queue and databases are not flooded, with **throttling** by provider limits, and a **kill switch** to stop a wrong campaign immediately.
- **Time-zone-aware sends** ("9 am local") spread the load across hours naturally.

## 9. Observability and operations

Track per channel and per provider: **send rate, success rate, delivery latency percentiles, bounce and complaint rates, queue depth and age of the oldest message**, DLQ size and push token invalidation rate ([observability](/sd/observability/logs-metrics-and-traces/)). Alert when the **oldest critical message** is older than its target, which is a user-visible symptom, instead of on raw queue length. Keep an **audit trail** of consent changes and sends (who, what, when), with personal data retention limits.

## Pitfalls

- Running marketing and critical messages through the same queue and workers.
- Retrying permanent failures (invalid tokens, bounced addresses) forever.
- Retrying without an idempotency key, so a timeout leads to duplicate sends.
- Ignoring the suppression list, or taking hours to honour an unsubscribe.
- Sending in the recipient's night because time zones were ignored.
- Letting one provider's outage block every worker (no circuit breaker, no timeouts).
- Unescaped template variables, which allow content injection in emails or in-app messages.
- No SMS fraud controls, leading to a surprise bill.
- Sending campaigns with no throttle and no kill switch.
- Measuring only "accepted by provider", not "delivered" and "complained".

## The problem

Design the **payment backend of an online marketplace**: shoppers pay for orders by card or wallet, the platform keeps the money safe, takes a fee, and later pays the sellers. We will integrate with an external **payment service provider (PSP)** such as Stripe or Adyen, which talks to the card networks and banks, and we will keep our own **ledger** of who owns what.

Unlike most case studies, this one is **not a throughput problem**. A busy platform handles a few hundred payments a second. The challenge is **correctness under failure**: a customer must never be charged twice, a payment must never silently disappear, and every cent must be traceable. Timeouts, retries, duplicate and out-of-order callbacks, and crashes in the middle of a multi-step operation are the normal conditions, not edge cases.

## 1. Requirements

**Functional**

- **Pay**: create a payment for an order, collect the customer's payment method, **authorize** and **capture** funds.
- **Refund** (full or partial) and handle **chargebacks** (disputes).
- **Payouts** to sellers, net of fees.
- A **ledger** with a complete audit trail, and statements per merchant.
- Process **webhooks** from the PSP (asynchronous results) and **reconcile** against its reports.

**Non-functional**

- **No double charges and no lost payments** (exactly-once *effect*), even with retries and crashes.
- **Money is conserved**: every movement has a matching opposite entry; balances can always be recomputed from history.
- **Strong consistency** for the ledger; availability matters but **never at the cost of wrong money**.
- **Auditability, security and compliance** (PCI DSS, strong customer authentication, data protection).
- **Graceful degradation**: if one PSP is down, fail over or queue, and tell the user honestly.

## 3. How a card payment actually works

1. The customer enters card details into a form **hosted by the PSP** (or a PSP-provided field in our page). The browser sends the card data **directly to the PSP**, which returns a **token**. Our servers never see the card number, which keeps most of the **PCI DSS** burden off us ([TLS and key management](/sd/security/tls-encryption-and-key-management/) covers the encryption side).
2. Our server creates a **payment** (often called a payment intent) at the PSP with the amount, currency, the token and an **idempotency key**.
3. The PSP asks the card network and the issuing bank to **authorize** the payment: the bank checks funds and risk, and places a hold. The customer may be asked for **strong customer authentication** (3-D Secure, a one-time code in the bank's app).
4. We **capture** the authorized funds (immediately, or later, for example when the item ships).
5. The PSP tells us the outcome through a **webhook** (an HTTP call to our endpoint), because many steps are asynchronous.
6. Days later the money is **settled**: the PSP pays out to our bank account, minus its fees, and sends a **settlement report**.

## 4. Idempotency: the first rule of payments

Networks lose responses. If the client sends a charge request and gets a **timeout**, it cannot tell whether the charge happened. Retrying blindly can charge the customer twice; not retrying can lose the sale. The answer is an **idempotency key**: a unique id the client generates for one logical operation and sends with every attempt. The server stores the **result against the key** and **replays** it if the key appears again.

The simulation charges 1,000 orders of 100 each. The provider **sometimes takes the money and loses the response** (a 30% chance), and the client retries after a timeout, up to five times.

<CodeRunner language="python" code={`import random

class PaymentProvider:
    """A charge API that always processes the request but sometimes loses the response (the client sees a timeout)."""
    def __init__(self, seed):
        self.rng = random.Random(seed)
        self.charges = []                    # the money actually taken
        self.by_key = {}
    def charge(self, amount, idempotency_key=None):
        if idempotency_key is not None and idempotency_key in self.by_key:
            outcome = self.by_key[idempotency_key]              # replay the stored result, do not charge again
        else:
            self.charges.append(amount)
            outcome = f"ch_{len(self.charges)}"
            if idempotency_key is not None:
                self.by_key[idempotency_key] = outcome
        if self.rng.random() < 0.3:
            raise TimeoutError("response lost")                  # the charge happened, but we never heard back
        return outcome

def checkout(provider, orders, use_key):
    for order in orders:
        key = f"order-{order}" if use_key else None
        for attempt in range(5):
            try:
                provider.charge(100, key)
                break
            except TimeoutError:
                continue                                         # the client retries

for use_key in (False, True):
    p = PaymentProvider(seed=4)
    checkout(p, range(1000), use_key)
    print(f"{'with' if use_key else 'without'} idempotency keys: 1000 orders of 100 -> {len(p.charges)} charges taken = {sum(p.charges):,} (should be 100,000)")
`} />

Without keys, 1,000 orders produced **1,436 charges and 143,600 taken instead of 100,000**: 436 customers charged twice or more. With keys, exactly **1,000 charges**. Rules for a good implementation:

- The **client generates the key** (a UUID per logical attempt, tied to the order) and reuses it on every retry, never a new one.
- The server stores `(key, request fingerprint, status, response)` **atomically with the work**, and if the same key arrives with a **different** request body, it rejects it.
- Keys expire after a window (for example 24 hours), long enough to cover retries.
- If the outcome is **unknown** (timeout, crash), the system must not assume failure: it **asks the PSP for the status** (by key or payment id) before deciding anything. See [delivery semantics and idempotency](/sd/queues-and-streaming/delivery-semantics-and-idempotency/).

## 5. A payment is a state machine, and webhooks are messy

A payment moves through states: `created` → `pending` → `authorized` → `captured` → (`refunded`), or `failed` at several points. Webhooks arrive **at least once, sometimes late, duplicated, or out of order** (a `captured` event can overtake `authorized`). A handler therefore must:

1. **Verify the signature** (an HMAC over the body with a shared secret, as in the [tokens lesson](/sd/security/tokens-oauth-and-jwt/)), and reject anything unsigned.
2. Be **idempotent by event id**: ignore duplicates.
3. **Never move backwards**: an old `authorized` arriving after `captured` is ignored.
4. If it sees a state that **skips steps** (a lost or delayed earlier event), accept it but **flag it** and reconcile later.
5. **Not trust the browser redirect** ("success" page) as proof of payment; only the webhook (or a direct status query) counts.
6. Keep a **polling fallback**: payments stuck in `pending` too long are checked against the PSP.

<CodeRunner language="python" code={`RANK = {"created": 0, "pending": 1, "authorized": 2, "captured": 3, "refunded": 4, "failed": 3}
ALLOWED = {("created", "pending"), ("pending", "authorized"), ("pending", "failed"), ("authorized", "captured"),
           ("authorized", "failed"), ("captured", "refunded"), ("created", "failed")}

def apply_events(events):
    state, seen, log = "created", set(), []
    for event_id, new_state in events:
        if event_id in seen:
            log.append(f"{event_id}: duplicate, ignored")
            continue
        seen.add(event_id)
        if new_state == state or RANK[new_state] < RANK[state]:
            log.append(f"{event_id}: stale '{new_state}' arrived after '{state}', ignored")
        elif (state, new_state) in ALLOWED:
            state = new_state
            log.append(f"{event_id}: {state}")
        else:
            state = new_state                                       # a skipped step: accept, but flag it for review
            log.append(f"{event_id}: {state} (skipped intermediate states, flagged)")
    return state, log

state, log = apply_events([("e1", "pending"), ("e3", "captured"), ("e2", "authorized"), ("e3", "captured"), ("e4", "refunded")])
print("final state after out-of-order and duplicate webhooks:", state)
print("\\n".join("  " + line for line in log))
`} />

The handler ended in the right place (`refunded`) despite the late `authorized` event and the duplicate `captured` one, and flagged the skipped step for review.

## 6. The ledger: double-entry bookkeeping

Do not store "the balance" as the source of truth. Store an **append-only ledger of entries**, and treat balances as **derived** (and cached) from it. Use **double entry**: every transaction consists of two or more entries that **sum to zero**, so money is only ever **moved**, never created or destroyed.

For a 100.00 purchase with a 2.90 fee, the entries might be: customer funds clearing `-100.00`, merchant payable `+97.10`, platform fee revenue `+2.90`. The sum is zero. A **refund** is not an edit: it is a **new** transaction with opposite entries. History is **immutable**; corrections are new entries.

Rules that prevent whole classes of bugs:

- **Money is an integer in minor units** (cents) plus a **currency code**, never a floating-point number.
- A transaction is **atomic**: all entries are written or none, in one database transaction ([ACID](/sd/databases-1/acid-and-transactions/)).
- **Invariants are enforced by the database**: a `CHECK (balance >= 0)` constraint, conditional updates, unique constraints for idempotency keys.
- **Never read, check in application code, then write.** Two concurrent requests can both pass the check. Do the check and the update in **one statement** (`UPDATE ... WHERE balance >= :amount`) or lock the row.

The code runs on SQLite, which is built into the browser runner. It transfers money atomically, rejects an overdraft with a conditional update, verifies that each transaction sums to zero, and shows what the naive read-then-write race does.

<CodeRunner language="python" code={`import sqlite3

db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
db.executescript("""
CREATE TABLE accounts(id TEXT PRIMARY KEY, balance INTEGER NOT NULL CHECK (balance >= 0));
CREATE TABLE entries(id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY AUTOINCREMENT, txn TEXT, account TEXT, amount INTEGER);
INSERT INTO accounts VALUES ('alice', 10000), ('bob', 0), ('merchant', 0);
""")
db.execute("INSERT INTO entries(txn, account, amount) VALUES ('opening', 'alice', 10000), ('opening', 'equity', -10000)")

def transfer(txn, src, dst, amount):
    """One database transaction: both legs are written, or neither is."""
    try:
        with db:
            cur = db.execute("UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance - ? WHERE id = ? AND balance >= ?", (amount, src, amount))
            if cur.rowcount == 0:
                raise ValueError("insufficient funds")
            db.execute("UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance + ? WHERE id = ?", (amount, dst))
            db.execute("INSERT INTO entries(txn, account, amount) VALUES (?, ?, ?), (?, ?, ?)", (txn, src, -amount, txn, dst, amount))
        return "ok"
    except ValueError as e:
        return str(e)

print("alice pays bob 8,000:", transfer("t1", "alice", "bob", 8000))
print("alice pays merchant 8,000 (only 2,000 left):", transfer("t2", "alice", "merchant", 8000))
print("balances:", dict(db.execute("SELECT id, balance FROM accounts")))
print("every transaction sums to zero:", all(total == 0 for (total,) in db.execute("SELECT SUM(amount) FROM entries GROUP BY txn")))

# The naive version reads the balance, checks it in application code, then writes. Two requests can interleave:
balance = 10000
read_a, read_b = balance, balance                      # both requests read 10,000 before either one writes
if read_a >= 8000:
    balance = read_a - 8000
if read_b >= 8000:
    balance = read_b - 8000
print(f"naive read-check-write, two 8,000 payments approved from a 10,000 balance: balance is {balance}, but 16,000 was paid out")
`} />

The overdraft was rejected by the database **in the same statement** that changed the balance, the second payment left no partial changes, and every transaction's entries summed to zero. The naive version approved both 8,000 payments from the same stale balance and ended at 2,000 after paying out 16,000: a **lost update**.

**Scaling the ledger.** At this volume a single well-run relational database (with replicas for reads) is enough for a long time. When you must shard, **shard by account** so most transactions are local. A transfer between accounts on different shards then needs coordination: use a **saga** with compensating entries ([distributed commit and sagas](/sd/distributed-theory/distributed-commit-and-sagas/)) or keep both legs in a platform-owned account on the same shard and settle between shards asynchronously. Do not reach for two-phase commit across services as a first answer.

## 7. Keeping the payment, the order and the event consistent: the outbox

A payment service typically has to do two things when a payment succeeds: **update its own database** and **tell the order service**. If it updates the database and then crashes before publishing the event, the order is never marked paid. If it publishes first and the database write fails, the order is marked paid for money it never recorded. There is no atomic "database and message broker" operation.

The **transactional outbox** pattern solves this: in the **same database transaction** that records the payment, insert a row into an `outbox` table describing the event. A separate relay process reads new outbox rows and **publishes them to the queue at least once**, marking them sent. Consumers (the order service) are **idempotent**, so duplicates are harmless. The state change and the intent to notify now commit or fail together.

## 8. Reconciliation: the safety net

Even with idempotency and a careful ledger, things diverge: a webhook is lost, a settlement amount differs because of fees or currency conversion, a chargeback arrives, a bug writes a wrong entry. **Reconciliation** compares your records with the external source of truth (the PSP's settlement report, and the bank statement) and flags every difference for investigation. It runs **daily** and is a core system, not a spreadsheet.

<CodeRunner language="python" code={`ours = {"p1": 5000, "p2": 1200, "p3": 7700, "p4": 300}      # payments in our ledger and their amounts (minor units)
theirs = {"p1": 5000, "p2": 1250, "p4": 300, "p5": 900}      # the provider's settlement report

print("reconciliation breaks:")
for pid in sorted(set(ours) | set(theirs)):
    if pid not in theirs:
        print(f"  {pid}: we recorded {ours[pid]} but the provider has no such payment -> investigate (never captured?)")
    elif pid not in ours:
        print(f"  {pid}: the provider settled {theirs[pid]} that we have no record of -> investigate (lost webhook or callback?)")
    elif ours[pid] != theirs[pid]:
        print(f"  {pid}: amounts differ, ours {ours[pid]} vs provider {theirs[pid]} -> investigate (fee, currency conversion or partial capture?)")
print("matched:", [pid for pid in ours if pid in theirs and ours[pid] == theirs[pid]])
`} />

Three categories of break appear: **missing at the provider**, **missing in our ledger**, and **amount mismatch**, each with a different likely cause and owner. Mature systems auto-resolve the known benign cases (a standard fee, a timing difference) and **alert on the rest**, tracking the age and total value of open breaks.

## 9. Refunds, payouts and disputes

- **Refunds** are new reversing transactions linked to the original payment, limited to the captured amount, and idempotent like charges. A partial refund reduces the merchant's payable and may or may not return the fee.
- **Payouts**: aggregate each merchant's payable balance, apply a holding period and minimum, send a bank transfer through the PSP or a payout API (again with an idempotency key), and record the entries when the bank confirms. Payout failures (closed account, wrong details) create **return** entries.
- **Chargebacks**: the cardholder disputes the payment with their bank; money is clawed back and a **fee** applies. Model them as state transitions with a deadline for evidence, and entries on the ledger.
- **Multiple currencies**: keep the **original currency and amount**, the conversion rate and the converted amount on the entry; never convert silently.

## 10. Security, compliance and fraud

- **Reduce PCI scope**: use PSP-hosted fields and tokens so card numbers never reach your systems; store only the token and the last four digits and brand. If you must handle card data, the compliance burden, network segmentation and encryption requirements are far heavier.
- **Encrypt** sensitive data at rest and in transit, restrict and **audit** access, rotate keys and secrets ([key management](/sd/security/tls-encryption-and-key-management/)).
- **Authenticate callers** and protect the payment API from abuse (rate limits, [web and API attacks](/sd/security/web-and-api-attacks/)).
- **Fraud and risk**: velocity limits (many payments from one device or card), address and device checks, risk scores that can trigger extra authentication or manual review. Fraud decisions should not break correctness: a payment held for review is a state, not an error.
- **Compliance** with local rules (strong customer authentication in many regions, anti-money-laundering checks, data residency) shapes workflows and must be designed with legal advice.

## 11. Availability and observability

- **Multiple PSPs**: route by method, country and cost, and fail over when one has an outage, keeping idempotency per provider (a retry on a different provider could double-charge: record which provider is authoritative for each payment).
- **Timeouts and circuit breakers** around the PSP ([retry amplification and circuit breakers](/sd/microservice-patterns/retry-amplification-and-circuit-breakers/)); degrade by offering other payment methods or accepting the order as "payment pending".
- **Key metrics**: payment success and authorization rates (by PSP, method, country), latency, webhook lag, payments stuck in `pending`, outbox backlog, **ledger imbalance (must be exactly zero)**, number and age of reconciliation breaks. Alert on the ones that mean money is wrong ([SLOs and alerting](/sd/observability/slos-error-budgets-and-alerting/)).
- **Runbooks** for PSP outages, duplicate-charge reports and reconciliation breaks, and regular drills.

## Pitfalls

- Using floating-point numbers for money.
- Treating a timeout as a failure, or retrying without an idempotency key.
- Checking a balance in application code and then writing (lost updates and double spends).
- Marking an order paid from the browser's success redirect instead of a verified webhook or status query.
- Webhook handlers that are not idempotent, not signature-verified or assume ordering.
- Editing or deleting ledger entries instead of adding reversing ones.
- Publishing events outside the database transaction (no outbox).
- Storing card numbers or logging full payment details.
- No reconciliation, so silent drift is discovered by customers or auditors.
- Retrying on a second PSP without knowing whether the first one succeeded.
- Ignoring currencies, rounding rules and time zones in settlement.

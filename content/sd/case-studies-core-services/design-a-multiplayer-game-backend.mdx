## The problem

Design the **backend of an online multiplayer game**: millions of players, matches of 2 to 100 people, where **every player sees a shared world that reacts in real time to everyone's actions**. Players expect to be put into a **fair, fast match**, to feel their own controls respond **instantly**, to see others move **smoothly**, to be protected from **cheaters**, and to keep their **progress** (rank, items) safely.

Games are an unusual distributed system: the deadlines are **tens of milliseconds**, the network is unreliable and **different for every player**, the clients are **untrusted** (players modify them), and the interesting state lives in **memory on short-lived servers**, not in a database.

## Requirements

**Functional**
- Players log in, see their profile, friends and inventory.
- **Matchmaking**: group players into fair matches quickly, by skill, region and game mode (parties of friends stay together).
- **Real-time play** inside a match: movement, actions, hit detection, game rules.
- **Persistence**: ranks, rewards and purchases are saved reliably after a match.
- Leaderboards, chat, reporting of cheaters, and regular live updates.

**Non-functional**
- **Latency**: round trip of 100 ms or less for most players; server simulation 20 to 60 times per second.
- **Fairness**: no player gains an advantage by cheating or by having a better connection than necessary.
- **Scale**: 1 million concurrent players, with large spikes at launches and events.
- **Availability and cost**: game servers are expensive compute; idle capacity wastes money, missing capacity creates queues.

Two lessons: the **real-time load is per match** (independent of other matches, so it parallelises perfectly), and **bandwidth per player** is the limit that forces cleverness in what is sent to whom.

- **Stateless services** (login, profile, store, matchmaking API) are built like any web backend: replicated, behind load balancers, with a database for durable data.
- **Game servers** are **stateful, short-lived processes**: one match runs entirely **in the memory of one process** until it ends. The **fleet manager** starts and stops them, tracks capacity per region, and hands matchmaking a server with room.
- A **match ticket** gives a client the server address and a **one-time token**, so only matched, authenticated players can connect.
- After the match the server sends a **result** to the results service: **idempotently** (keyed by match id), so a retry cannot award rewards twice ([delivery semantics](/sd/queues-and-streaming/delivery-semantics-and-idempotency/)).

## Real-time networking inside a match

### Authoritative server

The **server owns the truth**: clients send **inputs** ("move forward, fire"), the server simulates the world and sends back the **state**. A client is never trusted to report its position, health or hits, because the client belongs to the player and can be modified. **Server authority is the foundation of anti-cheat.**

### Tick rate and snapshots

The server advances the world in fixed steps (**ticks**), commonly 20 to 60 per second, and after each tick (or every few) sends each client a **snapshot** of the relevant state. To save bandwidth it sends **deltas** (only what changed since the snapshot the client last confirmed), and a full snapshot occasionally or after packet loss.

### Transport

Games typically use **UDP** (or a reliability layer built on it), not TCP: for fast-changing state, an **old position that arrives late is useless** and TCP's guarantee of in-order delivery would **hold back fresh data behind a lost packet** (head-of-line blocking). Critical events (a purchase, the end of the match) travel over a reliable channel. Browser games use WebSockets or WebRTC data channels ([real-time communication](/sd/networking-and-apis/real-time-communication/)).

### Hiding latency: prediction, interpolation, lag compensation

If the client waited for the server before showing its own movement, every key press would feel delayed by a full round trip. Games use three techniques:
- **Client-side prediction**: the client **applies its own input immediately** and also sends it; when the server's authoritative state arrives, the client **reconciles**: it re-applies any inputs the server has not yet processed on top of the server's state, and corrects itself if the prediction was wrong.
- **Entity interpolation**: other players' positions arrive only 20 to 60 times per second, so the client draws them **slightly in the past**, smoothly interpolating between the two latest snapshots instead of jumping.
- **Lag compensation**: when you shoot, the target you saw was where it was **100 ms ago** on your screen. The server **rewinds** the world to the time the shooter saw it (within a limit) to decide whether the shot hit. This favours the shooter and is a deliberate fairness trade-off.

### Interest management

A match of 100 players does not need to send every player everything. A player only needs the entities **near them** or otherwise relevant (the same idea as the spatial indexes in the [proximity service](/sd/case-studies-core-services/design-a-proximity-service/)). The server keeps a grid or region structure and sends each client only the **nearby** entities. This also hides information the player should not have (enemies behind walls), which reduces **wall-hack cheating**.

## Matchmaking

Putting players together is a **trade-off between quality and wait time**. Match only players of similar **skill** and each waits longer; match anyone and games are lopsided. The standard answer is a **widening search window**: begin strict, and **relax the allowed skill gap the longer a player waits**, up to a limit. Other constraints (region and latency, game mode, party size, role composition, avoiding recently played opponents) apply in the same way.

### The simulation

The program has three parts (standard library only, fixed seeds):

1. **Matchmaking policies** for a 1v1 game: players arrive randomly with a skill rating (average 1500, spread 300). Each second the matchmaker pairs players whose skill difference is within **both** players' allowed window. Three policies: **anyone** (no skill limit); **fixed** (always within 50 points); **expanding** (start at 50 points and widen by 20 points for each second waited, up to 400). Run for 10 minutes at **1 player per second** (a small population) and **10 per second**.
2. **Bandwidth** for state synchronisation in a 100-player match, with and without interest management.
3. **Input latency** with and without client-side prediction.

import math
import random


def simulate(rate, seconds, policy, seed):
    """Players arrive at random with a skill rating (mean 1500, spread 300) and wait to be paired.
    Every second the matchmaker pairs players whose skill difference is within BOTH players' allowed window."""
    rnd = random.Random(seed)
    t_next = rnd.expovariate(rate)
    waiting, matches, pid = [], [], 0                     # waiting: list of (skill, arrived_at, id)
    for sec in range(seconds):
        while t_next <= sec:
            waiting.append((rnd.gauss(1500, 300), t_next, pid))
            pid += 1
            t_next += rnd.expovariate(rate)
        waiting.sort()
        used = set()
        by_wait = sorted(waiting, key=lambda p: p[1])      # the longest-waiting players choose first
        for skill, arrived, i in by_wait:
            if i in used:
                continue
            w_me = window(policy, sec - arrived)
            best = None
            keys = [p[0] for p in waiting]
            pos = bisect.bisect_left(keys, skill)
            for j in range(max(0, pos - 6), min(len(waiting), pos + 7)):      # look at the nearest skills only
                s2, a2, i2 = waiting[j]
                if i2 == i or i2 in used:
                    continue
                if abs(s2 - skill) <= min(w_me, window(policy, sec - a2)):
                    if best is None or abs(s2 - skill) < abs(best[0] - skill):
                        best = (s2, a2, i2)
            if best:
                used.add(i)
                used.add(best[2])
                matches.append((abs(best[0] - skill), sec - arrived, sec - best[1]))
        waiting = [p for p in waiting if p[2] not in used]
    return matches, len(waiting), pid


def window(policy, waited):
    if policy == "anyone":
        return 1e9
    if policy == "fixed":
        return 50.0
    return min(400.0, 50.0 + 20.0 * waited)               # "expanding": start strict, relax by 20 points per second of waiting


def report(rate, policy, seconds=600, seed=1):

    unmatched = left

        "mean_gap": sum(gaps) / len(gaps), "bad": sum(g > 200 for g in gaps) / len(gaps), "left": unmatched, "players": total,
    }


if True:

## What the output shows

**Matchmaking quality against wait time**:

| arrivals | policy | mean wait | p95 wait | mean skill gap | matches with a gap above 200 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 per second | anyone | 1.0 s | 3 s | 302.7 | **54.1%** |
| 1 per second | fixed window of 50 | 9.2 s | **40 s** | 23.7 | 0.0% |
| 1 per second | **expanding** | **3.9 s** | **12 s** | 58.1 | 1.7% |
| 10 per second | anyone | 0.6 s | 1 s | 110.7 | 17.2% |
| 10 per second | fixed window of 50 | 1.5 s | 4 s | 21.2 | 0.0% |
| 10 per second | expanding | 1.2 s | 3 s | 27.4 | 0.0% |

- **Ignoring skill is fast and unfair**: with a small population more than **half** of matches (54.1%) have a skill gap above 200 points.
- **A strict fixed window is fair but slow when the population is small**: at 1 arrival per second the mean wait is 9.2 s and the **95th percentile 40 s**, because players at the extremes of the skill range (the rare very strong or weak) have few suitable partners.
- **An expanding window gets most of both**: at 1 per second a mean wait of **3.9 s** (p95 12 s) with a mean gap of 58 points and only 1.7% of matches above 200. The strict window's long tail is traded for slightly wider gaps.
- **Population matters more than policy**: with **10 arrivals per second** even the strict window waits only 1.5 s on average. A **bigger pool makes good matches cheap**, which is why small games, off-peak hours and rare skill levels are the hard cases, and why matchmakers adapt thresholds by time of day and mode.

**Bandwidth.** Sending every player's 40-byte state to every player at 30 ticks per second in a 100-player match costs **120 KB per second per player (0.96 Mbit/s)** and **12 MB per second out of the server**. With **interest management** (the 10 nearest players) it is **12 KB per second per player** and **1.2 MB per second** from the server: **10 times less**. Per-match server costs are dominated by this fan-out.

**Latency.** With a 100 ms round trip and a 30 Hz server tick, **waiting for the server before showing a move takes about 117 ms on average**: clearly noticeable. With **client-side prediction** the move shows within a frame (about 17 ms at 60 frames per second); the server's confirmation only corrects rare disagreements.

The player skills, arrival rates and the bandwidth and latency parameters are assumptions; the matchmaking numbers come from one seeded run of a deliberately simple 1v1 model.

## Fleet management

- **Regions**: place servers close to players; a player's **ping to each region** (measured by the client) is an input to matchmaking, which **prefers low latency** and relaxes it only as the wait grows.
- **Allocation**: a game server **process** is allocated per match (or per few matches) from a **warm pool**, so players do not wait for a machine to boot. The fleet manager scales the pool using **demand forecasts** (daily and weekly cycles, events) and the **matchmaking queue length**, with headroom for launches.
- **Failure**: if a game server crashes, the match is **lost** (state was only in memory). Mitigations: robust, well-tested server code; **automatic restart** of non-critical modes; **no rank loss** for interrupted matches; periodic **snapshots** to allow resumption for long sessions (rare, costly).
- **Draining** servers for updates: stop sending new matches, let current ones finish ([safe deployments](/sd/observability/safe-deployments-canaries-and-feature-flags/)).
- **DDoS**: game servers are attack targets; hide server addresses until match time, use provider protection, and rate-limit packets.

## Persistence and economy

- **Durable data** (profiles, inventory, purchases, rank) lives in **databases** behind the player service, sharded by player id. **Write after the match**, not on every action in it.
- **Idempotent results and rewards**: keyed by match id and player id so retries cannot duplicate rewards.
- **Purchases and currency** need **transactions and an audit log**: they are real money. Server-side validation only; the client never decides what it owns.
- **Leaderboards** use sorted structures ([real-time leaderboard](/sd/case-studies-data-and-transactions/design-a-real-time-leaderboard/)); ranks are updated from validated match results.
- **Unique ids** for players, matches and items ([unique ID generator](/sd/case-studies-data-and-transactions/design-a-unique-id-generator/)).

## Cheating

- **Never trust the client**: validate movement speed, fire rate, resources and positions on the server.
- **Interest management** removes information a wall-hack would reveal.
- **Statistical detection** on telemetry (impossible accuracy, reaction times, movement patterns), reviewed and acted on **after** the match, usually with **delays and waves of bans** so cheat makers cannot tell what was detected.
- **Client integrity** tools (anti-tamper software) raise the cost of cheating but **cannot be relied on**, since the client is in the attacker's hands; server authority is the real defence.
- **Report and review** pipelines for human reports, with abuse protection against false mass reporting.

## Pitfalls

- **Trusting the client** for positions, damage or inventory.
- **Using TCP for fast state** and suffering stalls after packet loss.
- **No prediction**: controls feel sluggish by a full round trip (about 117 ms in the example).
- **Sending everyone everything**: bandwidth and cheating exposure.
- **Strict matchmaking with a small population**, causing long tail waits.
- **Matching by skill but ignoring latency**, so the "fair" match is unplayable.
- **Persisting state on every game action**, overloading the database.
- **Non-idempotent reward grants**: double rewards on retry.
- **Cold-starting servers on demand** at peak, so players wait.
- **Ignoring crashes**: players lose rank for matches the server killed.
- **Revealing server addresses** early, inviting attacks.
- **Immediate bans** that teach cheat authors what is detected.

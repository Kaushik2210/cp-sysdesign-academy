## The problem

Design a **video streaming service** like YouTube or Netflix: creators (or the studio) **upload** videos, the platform **processes** them, and viewers around the world **watch** them on phones, TVs and browsers with fast start, little buffering and good quality, whatever their network does.

Three facts shape everything:

1. **Video is huge.** One hour of high-quality video is gigabytes; a busy service moves petabytes a day. **Bandwidth and storage cost** dominate the budget.
2. **Viewers' networks vary and fluctuate**, so one fixed file cannot give everyone a good experience. The solution is **adaptive streaming** with several versions of each video.
3. **Popularity is extremely skewed**: a small fraction of videos produce most of the views, which makes **caching** the central design tool.

## 1. Requirements

**Functional**

- **Upload** videos (large files, flaky connections), with resumable uploads.
- **Process** them into formats playable everywhere, plus **thumbnails**, subtitles and audio tracks.
- **Play** videos with adaptive quality, seeking and resume.
- Basic **metadata** (title, description), view counts, and access control (private, region-locked, paid).
- (Out of scope here: search, recommendations, comments, live streaming. Live is called out at the end.)

**Non-functional**

- **Playback**: fast start (about a second or two), very little rebuffering, quality that adapts to the network.
- **Global scale** with **cost efficiency**: serving bytes cheaply is the business.
- **Durability** of originals (never lose an upload); **high availability** of playback.
- **Content protection** where required (DRM, expiring links).

## 2. Estimation

Assume 100 million daily viewers who each watch 40 minutes a day at an average of 3 Mbit/s, and 30,000 hours of new video uploaded per day.

The viewing side moves **about 90 petabytes a day, 8 Tbit/s on average and perhaps 21 Tbit/s at peak**, far more than any single data centre can serve: a **content delivery network (CDN)** is not an optimisation here, it **is** the delivery system. On the upload side, **every hour of video becomes about 15 GB of renditions**, around **450 TB a day**, and thousands of cores of transcoding. (Estimates like these are assumptions to state aloud; the shape matters more than the digits.)

## 3. High-level design

<Diagram kind="mermaid" code={`flowchart TD

**Upload path.** The client uploads the file in **chunks directly to blob storage** using pre-signed URLs, so large transfers never pass through application servers and an interrupted upload **resumes** from the last chunk. When complete, an event enters the processing queue and the video is marked `processing`.

**Processing path.** A **workflow orchestrator** runs a graph of tasks: validate the file, split it into chunks, transcode every chunk into every rendition in parallel, package the output, create thumbnails, extract audio and subtitles, then publish (the video becomes `ready` and playable). Each task is idempotent and retried on failure (see [queues and retries](/sd/queues-and-streaming/backpressure-retries-and-dlq/)).

**Playback path.** The player asks the playback API for the video's **manifest URL** (after an access check). It fetches the manifest and then **small video segments** from the CDN. The origin storage is touched only on cache misses.

## 4. Adaptive bitrate streaming

Instead of one big file, each video is encoded into **several renditions** (a **bitrate ladder**: 240p, 360p, 480p, 720p, 1080p, 4K) and each rendition is cut into **segments** of a few seconds. A **manifest** (an HLS `.m3u8` playlist or a DASH `.mpd` file) lists the renditions and their segments. The **player** measures how fast segments arrive and how full its buffer is, and **chooses the rendition of every next segment**. Because segments are just static files served over ordinary HTTP, the whole thing runs on standard CDNs and caches ([HLS is specified in RFC 8216](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8216)).

The simulation compares three players on a deliberately poor, fluctuating network: one that **always asks for the top quality**, a **rate-based** player (choose the best rendition below 80% of the last measured throughput) and a **buffer-based** player (choose by how many seconds are buffered: low buffer means low quality).

<CodeRunner language="python" timeoutMs={30000} code={`import random

LADDER = [0.3, 0.75, 1.2, 2.5, 4.5, 8.0]            # Mbit/s renditions
SEG = 4.0                                             # seconds of video per segment
rng = random.Random(9)
trace, level = [], 5.0
for sec in range(600):                                # throughput per second: a random walk with sudden drops
    level = max(0.4, min(12.0, level + rng.uniform(-0.8, 0.8)))
    if rng.random() < 0.01:
        level = rng.uniform(0.4, 1.0)
    trace.append(level)
print(f"network: mean {sum(trace) / len(trace):.1f} Mbit/s, below 1 Mbit/s for {sum(1 for x in trace if x < 1) / len(trace):.0%} of the time, never above {max(trace):.1f}")

def download_time(start, megabits):
    """Seconds needed to download \`megabits\` starting at time \`start\` on the throughput trace."""
    t, remaining = start, megabits
    while remaining > 0:
        rate = trace[min(int(t), len(trace) - 1)]
        step = min(1.0 - (t - int(t)), remaining / rate)
        remaining -= rate * step
        t += step
    return t - start

def play(policy, segments=120):
    t, buffer, rebuffer, last_rate, history, switches = 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 5.0, [], 0
    for _ in range(segments):
        idx = policy(buffer, last_rate)
        if history and idx != history[-1]:
            switches += 1
        history.append(idx)
        d = download_time(t, LADDER[idx] * SEG)
        last_rate = LADDER[idx] * SEG / d
        if d > buffer:
            rebuffer += d - buffer if t > 0 else 0          # the startup wait is not counted as a stall
            buffer = 0.0
        else:
            buffer -= d
        buffer += SEG
        t += d
        if buffer > 30:                                      # the player pauses downloading when it has 30 s buffered
            t += buffer - 30
            buffer = 30
    return sum(LADDER[i] for i in history) / len(history), rebuffer, switches

def always_highest(buffer, last_rate):
    return len(LADDER) - 1

def rate_based(buffer, last_rate):                           # the highest rendition below 80% of the last measured throughput
    ok = [i for i, r in enumerate(LADDER) if r <= 0.8 * last_rate]
    return ok[-1] if ok else 0

def buffer_based(buffer, last_rate):                         # map the buffer level to a rendition: low buffer -> low quality
    if buffer < 8:
        return 0 if buffer < 4 else 1
    top = len(LADDER) - 1
    return min(top, 1 + int((buffer - 8) / 22 * top))

print("120 segments (8 minutes of video):")
for name, policy in (("always the highest quality", always_highest), ("rate-based", rate_based), ("buffer-based", buffer_based)):
    avg, stalls, switches = play(policy)
    print(f"  {name:28s} average bitrate {avg:4.2f} Mbit/s   stalled {stalls:6.1f} s   quality switches {switches:3d}")
`} />

The network averages only **2.0 Mbit/s** and falls below 1 Mbit/s about a fifth of the time. The results:

- **Always the highest quality (8 Mbit/s)** looks great on paper and is unwatchable: it **stalled for 869 seconds** in an 8-minute video.
- The **rate-based** player never stalled and averaged **1.19 Mbit/s**, but **switched quality 56 times**, because it reacts to every throughput wobble and its estimates come from short, noisy downloads.
- The **buffer-based** player also never stalled, averaged a higher **1.95 Mbit/s** and switched **48 times**: using the buffer level as the signal is more stable than guessing the throughput.

Real players combine both signals, limit the **frequency of switches** (visible quality flicker annoys viewers), use **small first segments and a conservative start** for fast startup, and **prefetch** the next segments. The architectural point: **the intelligence lives in the client**, so the servers stay simple, stateless and cacheable.

## 5. Transcoding

**What gets produced.** For each video: renditions across the bitrate ladder (and codecs: **H.264/AVC** plays everywhere, **VP9, HEVC and AV1** compress better, saving bandwidth for viewers with capable devices, but cost much more CPU and may carry licensing), audio tracks, subtitles, thumbnails and previews. Better services tune the ladder **per title** (a cartoon needs far fewer bits than a sports match for the same quality).

**It is a very parallel job.** Split the video at keyframes into chunks of about 10 seconds, transcode all chunks (and renditions) on a fleet of workers, then **concatenate and package**. The simulation shows how makespan falls as workers are added: one 2-hour video, one rendition, chunks taking about 1.5 times their duration to encode with some natural variation, plus 20 seconds for splitting, merging and verification.

<CodeRunner language="python" code={`import heapq, random

def makespan(video_seconds, chunk, workers, seed=1):
    r = random.Random(seed)
    n = int(video_seconds / chunk)
    times = [chunk * 1.5 * r.lognormvariate(0, 0.25) for _ in range(n)]       # a chunk takes about 1.5x its duration to encode
    free = [0.0] * workers
    heapq.heapify(free)
    for t in times:
        heapq.heappush(free, heapq.heappop(free) + t)
    return max(free) + 20                                                    # 20 s to split, merge and verify

video = 2 * 3600
sequential = makespan(video, video, 1)
print(f"transcoding a {video // 3600}-hour video into one rendition:")
print(f"  as one job on one machine      : {sequential / 60:6.1f} minutes")
for chunk, workers in ((60, 10), (10, 10), (10, 100), (10, 1000)):
    t = makespan(video, chunk, workers)
    print(f"  {chunk:3d} s chunks on {workers:4d} workers: {t / 60:6.1f} minutes ({sequential / t:5.1f}x faster)")
`} />

A 2-hour video that takes about **210 minutes** as one job finishes in about **19 minutes on 10 workers** and in about **2.4 minutes on 100**; at **1,000 workers** it needs only about **1 minute**, where the fixed 20-second split-and-merge step and the single longest chunk start to dominate, so returns diminish (an Amdahl's-law effect). Design consequences:

- **Chunk-level tasks are idempotent and retryable**: if a worker dies, only that chunk is redone.
- **Prioritise**: new uploads from popular channels and short videos first, the long tail later; use **spot or preemptible machines** for the bulk of the work.
- **Do not pre-compute everything for everything.** Transcode the common renditions up front and **create rarely needed renditions (4K, odd codecs) lazily** or only for videos that become popular.
- Keep the **original**: when a better codec arrives, you can re-encode the catalogue.

## 6. The CDN and caching

Serving 20 Tbit/s from the origin is impossible, so segments are served from **CDN edge caches** near viewers. Popularity follows a **heavy-tailed (Zipf-like) distribution**, which is why caching works so well even with modest cache sizes. The simulation requests videos from a 100,000-video catalogue with Zipf popularity through an LRU cache of several sizes.

<CodeRunner language="python" timeoutMs={60000} code={`import random
from collections import OrderedDict

random.seed(5)
catalog = 100_000
weights = [1 / (rank + 1) for rank in range(catalog)]          # Zipf: the k-th most popular video has popularity 1/k
requests = random.choices(range(catalog), weights, k=300_000)
print(f"CDN edge cache, {catalog:,} videos with Zipf popularity, {len(requests):,} requests:")
for pct in (0.001, 0.01, 0.05, 0.10):
    size, cache, hits = int(catalog * pct), OrderedDict(), 0
    for v in requests:
        if v in cache:
            hits += 1
            cache.move_to_end(v)
        else:
            cache[v] = True
            if len(cache) > size:
                cache.popitem(last=False)
    print(f"  cache holding {pct:6.1%} of the catalog ({size:6,} videos): hit ratio {hits / len(requests):6.1%}")
`} />

A cache holding just **0.1% of the catalogue served 29% of requests**, **1% served about 51%**, and **10% served 73%**. The curve flattens quickly, which defines the strategy:

- **Hierarchy**: edge caches (many, small, near viewers) in front of **regional caches** and an **origin shield** (one tier that collapses many edge misses into one origin request), so the origin sees only the long tail.
- **Place content proactively**: push the new hit show or a trending video to edge caches **before** demand arrives (large services put servers inside ISP networks and fill them during off-peak hours).
- The **long tail** (rarely watched videos) mostly misses and travels from origin; keep it on cheaper storage and accept a slightly slower start for those.
- **Cache keys** are segment URLs, immutable and versioned, so they never need invalidation, only expiry; use long TTLs.
- Watch **hit ratio, origin egress, and cost per delivered GB** as the headline metrics ([observability](/sd/observability/logs-metrics-and-traces/)).

## 7. Fast start and smooth seeking

- Start with a **low or medium rendition and small first segments**, then climb; start fetching the manifest and first segments as soon as the page loads (**preconnect**, prefetch).
- Align **keyframes** across renditions so the player can switch quality at a segment boundary without glitches and seek quickly.
- Serve manifests and segments from the **same CDN** with HTTP/2 or HTTP/3 and TLS session resumption.
- **Resume position** is stored per user and device (a small key-value write, batched).

## 8. Access control and content protection

- **Signed, expiring URLs** (or cookies) for segments stop casual hotlinking and sharing of private videos; the token is checked at the CDN edge.
- **DRM** (Widevine, FairPlay, PlayReady) encrypts segments and delivers keys only to licensed players, for studio content. It adds complexity and per-platform work, so reserve it for content that requires it.
- **Geo and rights restrictions** per region and time window in the metadata.
- **Upload abuse**: scan for malware, enforce size and format limits, use fingerprinting to detect known copyrighted material, and rate limit uploads.

## 9. Metadata, counters and analytics

- **Metadata** (title, owner, state, renditions available) lives in a sharded database or key-value store with caching; reads vastly outnumber writes.
- **View counts** at this scale are **not** an `UPDATE views = views + 1` on a row: viewers' players send **events** to a stream, a processor aggregates them in windows, and counters are updated in batches and approximated for popular videos ([hot keys](/sd/databases-2/hot-keys-and-hot-partitions/)). Exactness is neither necessary nor affordable for display counts; billing-grade counts use a separate, deduplicated pipeline.
- **Playback quality analytics** (startup time, rebuffer ratio, bitrate, errors per region and ISP) feed alerts and capacity decisions ([SLOs](/sd/observability/slos-error-budgets-and-alerting/): rebuffer ratio and start time are natural SLIs).

## 10. Live streaming is a different problem

Live video cannot be transcoded ahead of time. The pipeline ingests a stream (RTMP, SRT or WebRTC), transcodes **on the fly** into the ladder, packages **short segments (2 seconds or less for low latency)** and pushes them through the CDN while viewers are already watching. Latency, not cost, is the constraint: each stage adds delay, CDNs cannot cache a segment before it exists, and a single failure is visible to everyone immediately. Treat it as its own design with redundant ingest and transcoding paths.

## Pitfalls

- Streaming the original upload without transcoding, or only one quality.
- Uploading through application servers instead of directly to blob storage with resumable chunks.
- Pre-computing every rendition for every video, including ones never watched.
- No origin shield, so a viral video's cache misses stampede the origin.
- Players that chase every throughput fluctuation and flicker between qualities.
- Non-idempotent transcoding tasks that produce duplicate or corrupt output on retry.
- Counting views with a single hot row.
- Long, personalised or non-immutable segment URLs that destroy the cache hit ratio.
- Ignoring the cost per delivered GB until the first big bill.
- Treating live streaming as "just faster VOD".

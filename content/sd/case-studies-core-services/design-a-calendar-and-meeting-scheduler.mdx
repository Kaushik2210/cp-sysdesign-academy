## The problem

Design a **calendar service** like Google Calendar or Outlook: users create **events** (one-off and **recurring**), **invite** others, see a **day, week or month view** of their own and shared calendars, get **reminders**, book **meeting rooms**, and ask "**when are all of us free?**".

Calendars look simple and are full of traps that are not about scale at all: **time zones and daylight saving**, **recurrence with exceptions** ("every Monday, except this one, and this other one moved to 11:00"), **intervals** (overlap, merge, find gaps), and **permissions** on shared data. The scale problem (hundreds of millions of users) is mostly a **read** problem.

## Requirements

**Functional**
- Create, edit and delete events; **recurring** events with exceptions (cancel or change a single instance, or "this and all following").
- Invite attendees, track **RSVPs**, show conflicts.
- View a calendar for a date range; show **free/busy** for others without revealing details.
- **Find a time** that suits several people (and a room).
- Reminders by notification and email.
- Share calendars with permissions; sync with other clients (iCalendar, CalDAV).

**Non-functional**
- Calendar views load in **under 200 ms**.
- **Correct times** everywhere, including across daylight saving changes and time zones.
- Booking a **room** must never double-book.

## Estimation

| quantity | assumption | result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Users | 500 million | |
| Calendar views | 10 per user per day | 5 billion per day, about 58,000 per second, 174,000 at 3 times peak |
| Event writes | 1 per user per day (create, edit, RSVP) | about 5,800 per second |
| Stored events | about 200 per user, recurring series stored as one row | about 100 billion rows, about 30 TB at 300 bytes |

The ratio of reads to writes is about 10 to 1, and reads touch **one user's data** (plus the calendars shared with them), so **sharding by user or calendar id** works well and most queries stay on one shard.

## Data model

| table | key fields |
| --- | --- |
| **calendar** | id, owner, name, time zone |
| **event** (series master or single event) | id, calendar id, title, **start**, **end**, **time zone id**, **recurrence rule** (or none), series end date, organiser |
| **event_exception** | series id, **original start** of the instance, status (cancelled or modified), overriding fields (new start, title, ...) |
| **attendee** | event id, user, RSVP status |
| **acl** | calendar id, grantee, permission (free/busy only, read, edit) |

The decisions that matter:

1. **A recurring series is stored as a rule, not as rows per occurrence.** "Every Monday and Wednesday at 09:00, forever" is one row with a recurrence rule (the iCalendar **RRULE** format). Materialising every occurrence is impossible for an unbounded series and expensive to edit. The service **expands** the rule for the requested window at read time.
2. **Exceptions are separate records**, keyed by the **original start** of the instance they override. Expansion generates the instances, then removes cancelled ones and replaces modified ones.
3. **One-off events store an absolute instant (UTC) and recurring events store local time plus a time zone id.** Why the difference is the next section.

## Time zones and daylight saving

A meeting "every Monday and Wednesday at 9:00 in New York" means **9:00 on the wall clock in New York**, whatever the offset from UTC is on that date. In winter that is 14:00 UTC, in summer 13:00 UTC. If you convert the first occurrence to UTC once and **repeat that UTC time every week**, the meeting silently **moves by an hour** for everyone when the clocks change.

Rules that avoid this:
- **Store the rule in the event's own time zone** (as an IANA zone id such as America/New_York, not a fixed offset), and **expand each occurrence in that zone**, converting to UTC only for that instance.
- Keep the **time zone database current** (governments change rules, sometimes with weeks of notice) by updating it as part of the deployment.
- Handle **nonexistent local times** (02:30 on the night clocks go forward) and **ambiguous** ones (01:30 happens twice when they go back) with a stated policy, for example shift forward, and use the first occurrence.
- Display in the **viewer's** time zone, not the organiser's.
- One-off events can be stored as UTC because they happen once, but keep the original zone for display and for the "this and all following" edit.

## The simulation

The program uses only the Python standard library, including a small rule for the New York daylight saving dates, and runs two experiments:

1. **A weekly meeting** on Mondays and Wednesdays at 09:00 New York time, expanded across the **March 14, 2027** change, with one instance cancelled and one moved. It also shows what happens if the series had been stored as a **fixed UTC time**.
2. **Finding a common slot**: for 300 random days with 2 to 6 attendees, find the **earliest 60-minute gap** between 09:00 and 18:00 where nobody is busy, with a **sweep over merged busy intervals**, and check every answer against a **brute force** that tries every start minute against every meeting.

## What the output shows

**Daylight saving.** Stored as "09:00 New York time", the meeting converts to **14:00 UTC on March 8 and 10** (offset minus 5) and to **13:00 UTC from March 15** (offset minus 4, after the clocks changed on March 14): the UTC time changes, the wall-clock time does not. Stored as a **fixed 14:00 UTC**, attendees would see **09:00, 09:00, then 10:00 for every later occurrence**: the meeting drifts by an hour, which is exactly the bug users report every spring and autumn.

**Exceptions.** With the Wednesday March 10 instance **cancelled** and the Monday March 15 instance **moved to 11:00**, the expansion yields March 8 at 09:00, **March 15 at 11:00**, March 17, 22 and 24 at 09:00: the cancelled one is gone and the moved one is replaced, while the rest of the series is untouched.

**Free time.** On **300 random days** the sweep gave **the same answer as the brute force in all 300**, and a 60-minute common slot existed on **138** of them (46%: with 2 to 6 busy attendees and about 19 meetings a day in total, shared gaps are scarce). The sweep sorts the busy intervals once, merges overlaps and walks through the gaps: **O(m log m)** for m meetings, instead of testing 481 candidate start minutes against every meeting.

The meeting counts and gaps are synthetic and the daylight saving rule covers only New York in 2027; the lesson is the technique, not the percentage.

## Free/busy and finding a time

- **Free/busy**: for each attendee fetch their **busy intervals** in the window (from their calendars, expanding recurring events), **merge** them (sort by start, join overlapping intervals), and look for **gaps** at least as long as the meeting. For a room, add the room's calendar as another attendee.
- **Privacy**: other users should see only **busy or free**, not titles. The ACL has a "free/busy only" level, and the free/busy endpoint returns intervals without details.
- **Speed**: for people who are in many meetings, **cache each user's busy intervals per week** and invalidate when their events change. For large groups (50 attendees) fetch in parallel and merge; consider approximate "best slots" scores ("46 of 50 free") instead of a perfect answer.
- **Preferences**: working hours, time zones of attendees (avoid 03:00 for someone), and a minimum notice are applied as additional "busy" intervals.

## Storing and querying events

- **Index** events by `(calendar id, start time)` for range scans: "events in the week" fetches rows with `start < window end` and `end > window start`. A plain B-tree on the start time handles **single events** well when you also store a maximum event length or the end time in the index.
- **Recurring series** cannot be found by their `start` alone (the series started in 2022 and still recurs this week). Store a **series end date** (or "never") and query "series that started before the window end and have not ended before the window start", then **expand** those in memory. The number of series per user is small (dozens), so this is cheap.
- **Interval trees** answer "which intervals overlap this one?" in O(log n + k) for in-memory conflict checks and free/busy structures.
- **Shard by calendar or user id**; shared calendars add cross-shard reads, handled by fetching from each owner's shard and merging.

## Invitations, RSVPs and sync

- An invitation creates a **copy** of the event in each attendee's calendar linked to the organiser's event; RSVP updates go back to the organiser. Changes by the organiser are **propagated** to the attendee copies (asynchronously through a queue), except for attendee-specific fields such as their own RSVP and reminders.
- **External invitations** are emailed as **iCalendar** (.ics) messages with a method (request, reply, cancel); parse and apply incoming replies idempotently ([email service](/sd/case-studies-core-services/design-an-email-service/)).
- **Sync** with phones and other clients uses **sync tokens** or version numbers: the client asks "what changed since token T?" and receives only the deltas, including deletions (tombstones).

## Reminders

A reminder is "notify me 10 minutes before this event". Compute each upcoming reminder's **trigger time** from the event start (in UTC after expansion) and register it with a **scheduler** ([distributed job scheduler](/sd/case-studies-data-and-transactions/design-a-distributed-job-scheduler/)). Do not schedule an infinite series: **materialise only the next N occurrences' reminders** (for example the next 30 days) and a daily job extends the horizon. When an event changes, **cancel and reschedule** its reminders. Delivery is at-least-once, so the notification handler **deduplicates** by (event, instance, reminder).

## Room booking

A meeting room is a **resource** with its own calendar. Booking must be **atomic**: two people must not both get the 14:00 slot. Reuse the **conditional update** pattern from the [hotel booking case study](/sd/case-studies-data-and-transactions/design-a-hotel-booking-system/): reserve the room for the time slots in one transaction and fail if any slot is taken; or use a database **exclusion constraint** on time ranges where available. Recurring bookings expand into many slots and must be **all or nothing** (or report which instance conflicts).

## Pitfalls

- **Storing recurring events as fixed UTC**: the meeting moves at daylight saving.
- **Fixed offsets instead of IANA zone ids**; stale time zone data.
- **Ignoring nonexistent and ambiguous local times** at clock changes.
- **Materialising infinite series**.
- **Finding recurring events by their start date alone**.
- **Off-by-one on end times**: treat an event as the half-open interval from start up to but not including end, so back-to-back meetings do not conflict.
- **Leaking event details through free/busy**.
- **Non-atomic room booking** (check, then book).
- **Scheduling reminders for all future occurrences**.
- **Showing times in the organiser's zone** to attendees elsewhere.
- Treating **all-day events** as 24-hour intervals (they are dates, not instants, and span 23 or 25 hours on daylight saving days).

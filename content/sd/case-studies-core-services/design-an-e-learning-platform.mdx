## The problem

Design an **e-learning platform** like Coursera, Udemy or an internal training system: instructors publish **courses** made of **video lessons, readings and quizzes**; learners **enrol**, watch, answer questions, track their **progress**, earn **certificates**, and interact in discussions and live sessions.

A learning platform combines patterns you have seen elsewhere (video delivery, payments, search, notifications) with a few of its own:

- **Progress must never be lost**: a learner who finished 40 lessons and sees "0% complete" after an outage will leave. Progress is written constantly and from many devices.
- **Exams create spikes**: thousands of learners start the same timed quiz at the same moment.
- **Assessment must be trustworthy**: grades and certificates are credentials.
- The product goal is **learning**, not watching, so some features (review schedules, practice) are designed around **how memory works**.

## Requirements

**Functional**
- Browse and search a catalogue; **enrol** (free or paid).
- Watch video lessons with **resume from where you stopped**, on any device; download for offline use.
- Take **quizzes and exams** with immediate or later grading; practice questions.
- **Progress** per learner and course; completion rules; **certificates** that can be verified.
- Discussions, announcements, and **notifications**; instructor dashboards (completion, drop-off points, quiz statistics).

**Non-functional**
- **Video starts within 2 seconds** on a mediocre connection.
- **Progress durable** and consistent across devices within seconds.
- Handle **exam-start spikes** and end-of-term deadline rushes.
- **Accessible**: captions, transcripts, keyboard navigation, adjustable playback speed.
- **Privacy**: learner records are sensitive (especially for minors and employers).

### Content and video

Instructors upload source video; a **transcoding pipeline** produces **adaptive bitrate** renditions (360p to 1080p), thumbnails, **captions and transcripts** (generated, then edited), and stores segments in **object storage** behind a **CDN**. The player requests small segments and switches quality with the connection. **Offline downloads** are DRM-protected or time-limited encrypted copies. Because lecture content is **popular in bursts** (a course launches, a deadline approaches), the CDN absorbs nearly all of the load.

### Progress tracking

Progress has two parts:
- **Resume position** per lesson (how many seconds you watched): updated by a **heartbeat** every 10 to 30 seconds. Only the **latest value matters**, so each learner-lesson pair is a small record overwritten in place (last write wins, with a rule that position never goes backwards unless the learner seeks).
- **Completion events** ("finished lesson 7", "passed quiz 3 with 85%"): important facts that must **never be lost** or duplicated.

The client sends **events with a unique id**, to the progress service, which appends them to a **durable log** and updates the **latest-state store** (sharded by learner id). Properties that make this robust:
- **Idempotent**: replaying or duplicating an event does not change the result (the same discipline as in the [data pipeline lesson](/sd/case-studies-data-and-transactions/design-a-data-pipeline/)).
- **Order-insensitive**: each lesson keeps the **best** state (maximum position, completed stays completed), so late or out-of-order events from a phone that was offline are merged correctly.
- **Offline-first clients**: events are queued on the device and sent when the connection returns; the server accepts events with **old timestamps**.
- **Batch heartbeats**: to cut write volume, the client buffers and sends the latest position every 15 seconds rather than every second; the server **coalesces** heartbeats and writes **only meaningful changes** to the database.
- **Derived data from the log**: course completion percentage, streaks and instructor analytics are computed **from events**, so completion rules can change and be recomputed.

A demo in the simulation below shows that processing 20 events in order and the same events shuffled with 8 duplicates produce **identical** progress.

### Quizzes and exams

- **Question banks** with metadata (topic, difficulty); attempts draw questions at random with a fixed per-attempt seed, so refreshing does not change the questions.
- **Grade on the server**: the client receives the questions but **never the answers** (for auto-graded types); submissions are validated and graded server-side. Store every attempt and answer for audit and appeal.
- **Timing**: the server records the **start time** and enforces the deadline on **submission** (not on the client clock). Answers are **autosaved** frequently so a connection drop does not lose work.
- **Spikes**: 50,000 learners starting an exam at 09:00 means about 830 starts per second. Defences: **pre-generate or cache** question sets per exam version; **stagger** start windows; keep the exam path **independent** of heavy features (recommendations, analytics); **queue** non-critical work (certificate generation, emails) rather than doing it inline; load test at the expected peak with headroom.
- **Integrity**: randomised questions and order, per-attempt time limits, plagiarism and similarity detection on free text, proctoring options for high-stakes exams (privacy-sensitive). For open-book courses, design questions that test understanding, not recall.

### Certificates

A certificate is a **credential**, so it must be **verifiable** and **issued exactly once** per completion:
- Issuance is triggered by the **completion event** and is **idempotent** (keyed by learner and course) so retries do not produce duplicates.
- The certificate carries a **unique id** and a **digital signature** (or a stored hash), and a **public verification URL** where an employer can check it ([signatures and keys](/sd/security/tls-encryption-and-key-management/)).
- Revocation (cheating found later) is a state change in the verification record.

## Designing for learning: spaced repetition

People forget quickly after first learning and more slowly each time they **retrieve** the memory. Reviewing material **just before it would be forgotten** is far more efficient than re-reading it repeatedly or cramming. A learning platform can use this: schedule short **review questions** for each concept, **at increasing intervals**, and **adapt** the interval to how well the learner recalled it.

### The simulation

The program uses a deliberately simple **memory model** (the chance of recalling an item after t days is exp(minus t divided by its stability); each successful review multiplies the stability, by more when the item was nearly forgotten; a failed review cuts it). The constants are **illustrative**, not fitted to real learners. It compares schedules for 400 items learned on day 0 and tested on **day 90**: **cramming** (five quick repetitions on the first day), **daily** review, a **fixed widening schedule** (gaps of 1, 3, 7, 16, 35 and 70 days for everyone), and **adaptive** scheduling that reviews each item when its predicted recall falls to a target (95%, 85%, 70% or 50%). It then checks that **progress events** give the same result when duplicated and shuffled.

<CodeRunner language="python" timeoutMs={60000} code={`import math
import random

# A simple memory model: the chance of recalling an item t days after the last review is exp(-t / stability).
# Each successful review makes the memory last longer (more so when the review came after more forgetting),
# and a failed review makes it much shorter. These constants are illustrative, not fitted to real learners.


def recall_probability(days_since, stability):
    return math.exp(-days_since / stability)


def review(stability, days_since, rnd):
    """Do one review; return the new stability and whether the learner remembered."""
    r = recall_probability(days_since, stability)
    if rnd.random() < r:
        return stability * (1.05 + 3.0 * (1 - r)), True            # remembered: stronger, and more so if it was nearly forgotten
    return max(1.5, stability * 0.4), False                      # forgot: relearn from a weak memory


def simulate(schedule_name, items=400, horizon=90, final_test=90, seed=1):
    rnd = random.Random(seed)
    total_reviews = 0
    final_recall = 0.0
    for _ in range(items):
        difficulty = rnd.lognormvariate(0, 0.4)                 # some items are intrinsically harder to remember
        stability, last = 3.0 * difficulty, 0.0                 # first learned on day 0
        day, reviews = 0.0, 0
        while True:
            if schedule_name == "daily":
                nxt = day + 1
            elif schedule_name == "cram":
                nxt = day + (0.02 if reviews < 5 else 1e9)       # five quick repetitions on the first day, then never again
            elif schedule_name == "expanding":
                gaps = [1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 70, 140]                # a fixed, widening schedule, the same for every item
                nxt = day + gaps[min(reviews, len(gaps) - 1)]
            else:                                                # "adaptive:0.85": review when the predicted recall falls to 85%
                target = float(schedule_name.split(":")[1])
                nxt = day + stability * -math.log(target)
            if nxt > horizon:
                break
            day = nxt
            stability, _ok = review(stability, day - last, rnd)
            last = day
            reviews += 1
        final_recall += recall_probability(final_test - last, stability)
        total_reviews += reviews
    return final_recall / items, total_reviews / items


def progress(events):
    """Course progress from a stream of events that may arrive late, out of order or twice: keep each lesson's best state."""
    done, watched = set(), {}
    for e in events:                                            # e = (event_id, lesson, seconds_watched, completed)
        _, lesson, seconds, completed = e
        watched[lesson] = max(watched.get(lesson, 0), seconds)
        if completed:
            done.add(lesson)
    return done, watched


if True:
    print("400 flashcard-like items learned on day 0, tested on day 90 (memory model with assumed constants):")
    for name in ("cram", "daily", "expanding", "adaptive:0.95", "adaptive:0.85", "adaptive:0.70", "adaptive:0.50"):
        recall, reviews = simulate(name)
        print(f"  {name:14s}: average chance of recalling an item on day 90 {recall * 100:5.1f}% | reviews per item over 90 days {reviews:5.1f}")

    # progress events: duplicates and out-of-order delivery must not change the answer
    rnd = random.Random(4)
    events = [(i, f"lesson-{i % 5}", 60 * (i // 5 + 1), i // 5 == 3) for i in range(20)]
    shuffled = events + rnd.sample(events, 8)                   # eight duplicates
    rnd.shuffle(shuffled)
    a, b = progress(events), progress(shuffled)
    print(f"progress from 20 events in order versus 28 events shuffled with 8 duplicates: identical result: {a == b}; lessons completed {sorted(a[0])}")
`} />

## What the output shows

| schedule | average chance of recalling an item on day 90 | reviews per item over 90 days |
| --- | --- | --- |
| cramming (5 repetitions on day 0) | **0.0%** | 5.0 |
| daily review | 100.0% | **90.0** |
| fixed widening schedule | 13.1% | 5.0 |
| adaptive, review at 95% predicted recall | 97.4% | 34.4 |
| adaptive, review at 85% | **92.2%** | **17.7** |
| adaptive, review at 70% | 84.7% | 14.0 |
| adaptive, review at 50% | 73.6% | 14.8 |

- **Cramming produces nothing that lasts**: five repetitions at once leave essentially **0%** recall at day 90. Massed practice feels productive and is not.
- **Daily review works** but costs **90 reviews per item**, five times the 17.7 of an adaptive schedule that reaches **92.2%**. Reviewing items that are still well remembered adds little: the simulation's gain from a review is small when recall is still near 100%.
- **A fixed widening schedule can be badly matched** to the learner: with these (assumed) memory constants it recovered only **13.1%**, because the gaps were too long for these items and memories were lost between reviews. The right spacing depends on the **item and the learner**, so schedules should **adapt per item** based on performance.
- **There is a sweet spot**: waiting until recall has fallen to **50%** needed **more** reviews (14.8) than waiting until 70% (14.0) **and** left lower retention (73.6% against 84.7%), because items that were forgotten had to be relearned from a weak memory. Reviewing **too early wastes effort; too late wastes more**.
- **Retention costs reviews**: the 95% target buys 97.4% recall for 34.4 reviews, the 85% target 92.2% for 17.7: **diminishing returns**, so the target is a product decision about time versus retention.
- **Progress events**: 20 events in order and the same events with 8 duplicates, shuffled, give **identical** progress (all five lessons completed): the idempotent, order-insensitive merge works.

These numbers come from a **toy memory model** with made-up constants and one random seed: they demonstrate the **shape** of the trade-off (cramming fails, over-reviewing wastes time, too-late reviews backfire), not what a real learner's retention would be. Real systems fit models of this kind to data from millions of reviews.

## Instructor analytics

Instructors want to know **where learners drop off** (which lesson, which minute of which video), **which quiz questions** are too easy or confusing (everyone misses it; nobody misses it; the best learners miss it), and **completion by cohort**. These come from the **event log** through batch jobs and are stored as aggregates; heavy queries run on an **analytics store**, never on the live progress database ([data pipeline](/sd/case-studies-data-and-transactions/design-a-data-pipeline/)).

## Pitfalls

- **Losing progress**: single-copy, non-durable progress writes or non-idempotent updates.
- **Treating late events as errors** instead of merging them.
- **Computing completion in the client** only.
- **Sending quiz answers to the client**, or trusting the client clock for time limits.
- **Not load testing the exam start**, then watching it fail in front of 50,000 people.
- **Doing everything inline at submission** (certificate rendering, emails) instead of queueing it.
- **Duplicate or unverifiable certificates**.
- **Counting watch time as learning**: measure retrieval and performance, not minutes.
- **Mass cramming features** (streaks that reward volume over spaced practice).
- **Ignoring accessibility**: no captions, no keyboard control.
- **Exposing learner data** to instructors or employers beyond consent.
